\textbf{Abstract}

%ABSTRACT-BEGIN
In delayed perceptual reports, the decision is formed before action is permitted, yet response time from the response signal still depends on sensory evidence. This paradox raises the possibility that evidence accumulation leaves a graded trace in motor preparatory circuits rather than ending in a discrete stored choice. We tested this hypothesis in 22 human adults of both sexes (17 women) performing a response-signal random-dot motion task with electroencephalography and electromyography. Participants judged motion direction, withheld the selected response for 3 s, and executed it when the response signal was presented; motion coherence and duration were manipulated. Response time increased when coherence or duration decreased. Fractionating response time at muscle-activation onset showed that these effects were confined to the premotor period. Muscle activations that did not produce overt responses provided a peripheral readout of motor preparation: they were effector-selective, built up during stimulus presentation at a coherence-dependent rate, and continued to ramp during the stimulus-free delay, but no longer at a coherence-dependent rate. These results support an account in which accumulated motion evidence is transmitted to motor preparatory circuits, transformed into a sustained state maintaining the selected response, and shaped by temporal preparation before action is permitted. Electroencephalography revealed a hierarchy: mu--beta-band lateralization tracked motion-evidence accumulation and temporal preparation, whereas the lateralized readiness potential tracked temporal preparation and continued to build up toward the response. Thus, delayed decisions are preserved not as discrete stored choices, but as graded motor-preparatory states that remain dynamically regulated until action is permitted.
%ABSTRACT-END

\textbf{Significance statement}

Perceptual decisions often cannot be reported immediately, yet the brain must preserve them until action is allowed. By combining brain and muscle recordings in humans, we show that, over short delays, information extracted from a stimulus is not simply converted into a fixed choice and released later. Instead, it remains graded in motor preparatory circuits, where it is maintained and adjusted by expectations about when action can occur. Muscle activations too weak to produce overt responses provided an especially sensitive window onto these hidden preparatory states, offering a novel tool for studying how decisions are maintained and regulated before behavior.


\section{Introduction}

%INTRO-BEGIN
Perceptual decision-making transforms sensory information into choices that guide action. Much of the human literature uses paradigms in which participants report a choice as soon as they commit to it \parencites{forstmannSequentialSamplingModels2016}{oconnellNeurophysiologyHumanPerceptual2021}. This work has motivated neurally informed models in which noisy sensory evidence is accumulated and continuously conveyed to the motor system, allowing candidate actions to be prepared in parallel \parencites{balsdonSecondaryMotorIntegration2023}{dendauwGatedCascadeDiffusion2024}{kellyNeurocomputationalMechanismsPriorinformed2021}. However, commitment and action are often separated in time, raising the question of how motor preparation is preserved while a decision report is withheld.

A canonical paradigm for studying perceptual decision-making is the random-dot motion discrimination task, in which observers judge motion direction and report their choice with an eye or hand movement. Recent variants impose a fixed delay between stimulus offset and a response signal (RS), requiring observers to withhold their report until prompted \parencites{achardExtendingContinuousFlow2025}{roggeMotorCorticalSignals2022}. If sensory evidence yielded a categorical choice that was stored and merely released at the RS, mean response time (RT; measured from RS onset) should be insensitive to earlier stimulus characteristics. Yet mean RT is longer with lower stimulus discriminability or shorter stimulus duration, even across delays of up to seven seconds \parencite{achardExtendingContinuousFlow2025}. These effects suggest that prior sensory evidence continues to influence action after stimulus processing ends.

We propose that motor preparation preserves the level reached when evidence accumulation ends, retaining a graded trace of prior sensory evidence until report. Human electroencephalography (EEG) supports a motor locus for maintenance: when the stimulus--response mapping is known, effector-selective mu--beta signals persist until delayed report, whereas the centroparietal positivity, a candidate marker of abstract evidence accumulation, returns toward baseline after peaking \parencites{roggeMotorCorticalSignals2022}{twomeyAbstractEffectorSelectiveDecision2016}. We formalized this hypothesis by developing a delayed-report extension of the continuous-flow model of \textcite{dendauwGatedCascadeDiffusion2024}, which links evidence accumulation to motor preparation and, ultimately, muscle activation. Accumulation continues until motor preparation reaches an internal threshold or motion processing ends. The resulting motor-preparatory level is then maintained, with stochastic fluctuations, until responding is permitted. Weaker or briefer evidence is less likely to bring motor preparation to threshold, leaving motor preparation at a lower level on average. This graded maintenance is consistent with bounded integration under externally controlled stimulus durations \parencite{kianiBoundedIntegrationParietal2008} and with recurrent-circuit accounts linking accumulation and working memory \parencite{wangDecisionMakingRecurrent2008}. An inhibitory gate limits transmission of preparatory activity to the muscles, allowing preparation without overt movement \parencite{dendauwGatedCascadeDiffusion2024}. The RS engages a distinct stochastic process that accumulates noisy evidence of its presence, increasing preparation for the selected response. Although the RS is unambiguous, noisy temporal integration is plausible given accumulator accounts of one-choice RT \parencite{ratcliffDiffusionModelOnechoice2011} and EEG signatures of accumulation during near-ceiling target detection \parencite{oconnellSupramodalAccumulationtoboundSignal2012}. A lower maintained motor-preparatory level thus requires more RS-driven buildup before execution, prolonging RT.

Maintaining an evidence-dependent motor state does not imply that preparation remains static during the delay. Predictable timing of an opportunity to act can enhance preparation for the selected action \parencites{heidemanTemporalAlignmentAnticipatory2018}{liBetaOscillationsInternal2026}. We therefore examined a basic implementation with graded maintenance alone (Model 1) and a variant incorporating temporal preparation (Model 2). To test the architecture beyond behavior, we recorded surface electromyography (EMG) and EEG during motion judgments with a 3-s response delay, varying coherence and duration. EMG indexes motor preparation through \emph{partial muscle activations} (PMAs): transient muscle activations that do not produce an overt response \parencites[e.g.,][]{colesPsychophysiologicalInvestigationContinuous}{servantUsingCovertResponse2015}. Both models predict PMAs when motor-preparatory activity transiently overcomes the inhibitory gate without triggering a response. Model 1 predicts a constant PMA rate during the delay, whereas Model 2 predicts a rate that increases as the RS approaches. Mu--beta lateralization and the lateralized readiness potential provide complementary cortical measures of effector-selective preparation.
%INTRO-END


\section{Materials and Methods}


\subsection{Participants}

Twenty-two participants (17 women; mean age = 20.08; range 18--24) took part in the experiment in exchange for a \texteuro{}20 gift card. The sample size was informed by Experiment 2 of \textcite{achardExtendingContinuousFlow2025}, which provided strong Bayesian evidence (inclusion Bayes factor > 10) for effects of stimulus discriminability and duration on delayed-report latency. Participants were students at Universit\'e Marie et Louis Pasteur (France) recruited through online advertisements. All were right-handed, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and reported no history of motor, psychiatric, or neurological disorders. Participants were unaware of the study purpose and provided written informed consent. The university research ethics committee approved the study (Agreement CERUBFC-2022-01-18-002).


\subsection{Experimental task}

Participants performed an RS variant of the random-dot motion discrimination task (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}A). They judged whether motion was leftward or rightward and withheld their response until the RS appeared, 3 s after stimulus offset. Leftward and rightward judgments were reported with the corresponding thumb using two buttons mounted in a rubberized ergonomic grip (Black Box Toolkit). Motion duration (0.3 or 2 s) and coherence (0\%, 12\%, or 50\%) varied factorially. The 0\% condition contained no net directional evidence and therefore indexed chance-level choices, whereas 12\% and 50\% coherence provided intermediate and high discriminability, respectively.

The stimulus contained white dots (0.05$^{\circ}$ in diameter; 71 dots per frame on average) presented within a virtual circular aperture (11.78$^{\circ}$ in diameter) on a black background. On each frame, a proportion of dots moved coherently leftward or rightward at 10.5$^{\circ}$/s, whereas the remaining dots were repositioned randomly. Stimuli were displayed on a 34.42 $\times$ 19.36 cm LCD monitor (1920 $\times$ 1080; 60 Hz) viewed from 75 cm in a dimly lit room. Participants rested their hands on a cushion to minimize incidental muscle activity.

Participants completed 10 blocks of 96 trials, with self-paced breaks between blocks. Within each block, trials were defined by the factorial combination of motion direction, motion coherence, and motion duration (0.3 vs 2 s). Each trial type was presented eight times per block in randomized order, resulting in 160 trials per coherence $\times$ duration condition across the experiment. Each trial began with a colored fixation cross, which remained at the center of the screen throughout the trial. After a variable prestimulus interval (0.6, 0.8, or 1 s, randomly selected on a trial-by-trial basis), the motion stimulus appeared and was followed by a 3-s delay. A change in fixation color from blue to yellow served as the RS. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible after this change. If no response was made within 4 s of RS onset, the feedback message ``Too late, please respond faster'' was displayed for 2 s. If a response occurred before the RS, the feedback message ``Too early! Please wait for the color change of the fixation cross'' was displayed for 2 s. Feedback messages or valid responses were followed by a 1.5 s inter-trial interval, during which a schematic image of an eye was displayed. Participants were instructed to maintain fixation on the central cross and refrain from blinking until the eye schematic appeared. Trials with no response, a response before the RS, or an RT shorter than 0.2 s were excluded.


\subsection{Theoretical framework}

We formalized the delayed-report account by extending recent evidence-accumulation models that continuously link decision formation to motor preparation in immediate-report tasks \parencites{balsdonSecondaryMotorIntegration2023}{heitzNeuralMechanismsSpeedAccuracy2012}{verdonckLeakyIntegratingThreshold2021}, building in particular on the framework developed by \textcite{dendauwGatedCascadeDiffusion2024}, which extends this decision-to-motor architecture to muscle activation through an inhibitory gating mechanism. A key advantage of this framework is that it links latent motor-preparatory dynamics to overt responses and PMAs on individual trials, allowing EMG observations to directly constrain the model.

To accommodate delayed report, we introduced mechanisms for maintaining an evidence-dependent motor-preparatory state and initiating the selected response upon presentation of the RS. As outlined in the Introduction, we considered two versions of this extension: Model 1 includes graded maintenance alone, whereas Model 2 additionally incorporates temporal preparation for the anticipated RS. Both models share the same mechanisms of evidence accumulation, motor preparation, gating inhibition, and response initiation, differing only in the contribution of temporal preparation to the signal transmitted to the downstream gate. Below, we describe the inherited architecture before detailing these extensions. Throughout, time $t$ is measured from physical stimulus onset.


\subsubsection{Architecture inherited from immediate-report models}

The model assumes that a decision variable $x(t)$ represents accumulated evidence for the two motion alternatives. Following a fixed motion-evidence encoding latency (parameter $L_{\mathrm{motion}}$), it evolves according to:
\begin{equation}
\dd x(t)=skc\,\dd t+\sigma_{x}\,\dd W_{x}(t), \qquad x(0)=x_{0},
\label{eq:x}
\end{equation}
where $s\in\{-1,+1\}$ codes motion direction, $c$ is motion coherence, $k$ scales coherence into the mean rate of evidence accumulation (drift rate), $\sigma_{x}$ is the diffusion coefficient controlling within-trial noise, and $\dd W_{x}(t)$ is an increment of a standard Wiener process. Positive and negative values of $x(t)$ support rightward and leftward choices, respectively, by arbitrary convention. At the neurophysiological level, this process can be interpreted as the temporal integration of differences in activity between opposing populations of direction-sensitive neurons in area MT \parencites{goldNeuralBasisDecision2007}{mazurekRoleNeuralIntegrators2003}. Equation~\eqref{eq:x} provides a parsimonious implementation of evidence accumulation, although alternative dynamics, including leakage or mutual inhibition, could also be considered \parencites[e.g.,][]{servantNeurallyConstrainedModeling2019}{usherTimeCoursePerceptual2001}.

The decision variable $x(t)$ is continuously transmitted to a distinct motor-preparation variable $y(t)$:
\begin{equation}
\dd y(t)=\lambda\left[x(t)-y(t)\right]\dd t, \qquad y(0)=x_{0}.
\label{eq:y}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:y} describes a first-order low-pass filter through which motor preparation tracks the evolving decision variable while attenuating rapid fluctuations. Consequently, $y(t)$ provides a smoother and temporally lagged representation of accumulated evidence, whose mean buildup becomes slower as sensory-evidence strength decreases. The filter gain ($\lambda>0$) controls how rapidly $y(t)$ tracks changes in $x(t)$; lower values produce greater smoothing and a longer temporal lag. Positive and negative values of $y(t)$ increase preparation for the right- and left-hand responses, respectively. Evidence for continuous transmission from decision formation to motor preparation comes from human and monkey studies showing that developing motor commands, motor-cortical activity, and stretch-reflex gains track evolving sensory evidence rather than merely expressing a completed choice \parencites[e.g.,][]{donnerBuildupChoicePredictiveActivity2009}{goldRepresentationPerceptualDecision2000}{selenDeliberationMotorSystem2012}. Human EEG recordings further show temporally overlapping, evidence-dependent buildup of decision-related and motor-preparatory signals, with the latter lagging the former \parencite{kellyInternalExternalInfluences2013}. More specific support for the filtering operation comes from \textcite{balsdonSecondaryMotorIntegration2023}, who found that EEG-derived estimates of the temporal lag between evidence accumulation and motor preparation increased with the degree of temporal smoothing inferred from behavioral model fits. A related operation was incorporated into the integrated accumulator model of \textcite{heitzNeuralMechanismsSpeedAccuracy2012}, in which the accumulating activity of frontal eye field movement neurons is low-pass filtered to generate a downstream signal controlling saccade initiation.

Transmission from motor preparation to the muscles is also continuous but regulated by gating inhibition. The signed motor-preparation variable $y(t)$ is transformed into separate nonnegative neural drives for the left and right response agonists:
\begin{equation}
\left\{\begin{aligned} z_{L}(t)&=\max\left[-y(t)-g,0\right],\\ z_{R}(t)&=\max\left[y(t)-g,0\right],\end{aligned}\right.
\label{eq:z}
\end{equation}
where $g$ denotes the level of gating inhibition. Motor preparation must therefore overcome this inhibition before generating neural drive to the corresponding response muscle. Muscle activation begins when either $z_{L}(t)$ or $z_{R}(t)$ becomes positive. Producing an overt response, however, requires a sufficient level of muscle activation to generate the force needed to register the response. In the model, this requirement is represented by a response criterion $r$ applied to neural drive. When either neural drive reaches $r$, the overt response occurs after an additional residual motor latency $L_{\mathrm{motor}}$, which captures muscular and biomechanical contributions to response timing not explicitly represented by the model, including the development and transmission of force to the response device. Neural drive can nevertheless become positive and return to zero without reaching $r$; such transient gate crossings generate PMAs rather than overt responses.


\subsubsection{Extension to delayed report}

The delayed-report extension specifies the locus and onset of maintenance, the motor-preparatory level retained, and its subsequent dynamics. We then describe the additional contribution of temporal preparation in Model 2 and the RS-driven response-initiation process shared by both models. Throughout the delayed-report trial, gating inhibition continues to regulate transmission from motor preparation to neural drive, and an overt response is produced only when neural drive reaches the response criterion $r$. All model variables and parameters are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:params}.

\emph{Locus of maintenance.} Delayed report requires information established during motion processing to remain available until the RS. In principle, this information could be maintained in the decision variable $x(t)$, in the motor-preparation variable $y(t)$, or in both. We implemented maintenance in the motor-preparation variable $y(t)$. As outlined in the Introduction, this choice was motivated by EEG evidence showing that the centroparietal positivity, a candidate neural signature of accumulated decision evidence, returns toward baseline after peaking, whereas effector-selective mu--beta activity persists during delayed report \parencites{roggeMotorCorticalSignals2022}{twomeyAbstractEffectorSelectiveDecision2016}.

\emph{Onset of maintenance.} For a motion stimulus presented from $t=0$ to $t=D$, encoded motion evidence is potentially available to the decision process from $L_{\mathrm{motion}}$ to $L_{\mathrm{motion}}+D$, where $L_{\mathrm{motion}}$ denotes the motion-evidence encoding latency \parencite[e.g.,][]{resulajChangesMindDecisionmaking2009}. If the magnitude of motor preparation reaches an internal threshold $\theta$ during this interval, motion-evidence accumulation terminates at threshold crossing and maintenance begins at that time, denoted $\tau$. This implements the bounded-accumulation account introduced in the Introduction. The threshold was constrained to lie below gating inhibition ($\theta<g$), so that maintenance could begin before motor preparation was sufficient to generate muscle activation. If $\theta$ was not reached before encoded motion evidence ceased, accumulation continued until $L_{\mathrm{motion}}+D$, and maintenance began at $\tau=L_{\mathrm{motion}}+D$.

\emph{Value retained at maintenance onset.} The motor-preparatory state established at the transition defines the maintenance target $m$:
\begin{equation}
m = \begin{cases} s_{\mathrm{resp}}\theta, & \text{if } \theta \text{ was reached},\\ y(L_{\mathrm{motion}}+D), & \text{otherwise},\end{cases}
\label{eq:m}
\end{equation}
where $s_{\mathrm{resp}}\in\{-1,+1\}$ codes the selected response, with $-1$ denoting the left-hand response and $+1$ the right-hand response, following the sign convention defined for $y(t)$. If the internal threshold $\theta$ is reached, $y(t)$ is set to the threshold value in the selected direction. Otherwise, the state attained when encoded motion evidence ceases is retained. Thus, the model does not require every trial to enter maintenance at the same level of motor preparation: weaker or briefer evidence can leave a lower maintained state.

\emph{Maintenance dynamics.} The maintenance mechanism is identical in the two candidate models. Once maintenance begins, the input to the low-pass filter generating $y(t)$ switches from the evolving decision variable $x(t)$ to the maintenance target $m$. Because the filter causes $y(t)$ to track its input, a constant input $m$ causes $y(t)$ to converge toward $m$ and remain there in the absence of noise. Such stable maintenance has a plausible neural basis in attractor dynamics, in which strong recurrent interactions can sustain a stable activity state after the external evidence driving the system is no longer present \parencite{wangDecisionMakingRecurrent2008}. At the same time, neural activity within such recurrent networks is intrinsically variable, making perfectly stationary maintenance at the single-trial level unlikely. To capture this variability without specifying its detailed circuit-level origin, we allowed the effective maintenance input to fluctuate around $m$. These fluctuations, denoted $\varepsilon(t)$, followed an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process:
\begin{equation}
\dd\varepsilon(t)= -\kappa_{\varepsilon}\varepsilon(t)\,\dd t+ \sigma_{\varepsilon}\,\dd W_{\varepsilon}(t), \qquad \varepsilon(\tau)\sim \mathrm{N}\left(0,\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{2\kappa_{\varepsilon}}\right),
\label{eq:epsilon}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_{\varepsilon}$ controls the rate at which deviations return toward zero, $\sigma_{\varepsilon}$ controls the magnitude of stochastic fluctuations, and $\dd W_{\varepsilon}(t)$ is an increment of a standard Wiener process. We drew $\varepsilon(\tau)$ from the stationary distribution of the process, independently of motion-evidence noise, to avoid an artificial reset of variability at maintenance onset. The resulting maintenance dynamics are therefore:
\begin{equation}
\dd y(t)=\lambda\left[m+\varepsilon(t)-y(t)\right]\dd t.
\label{eq:y_delay}
\end{equation}
The Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process provides a parsimonious description of temporally correlated fluctuations around the maintained state with finite stationary variance. Because deviations are driven back toward zero, stochastic perturbations do not accumulate indefinitely over the delay, as they would under a pure Wiener process. Consequently, $y(t)$ remains centered on $m$ in expectation while exhibiting temporally structured variability within individual trials. The fluctuation of the motor-preparatory signal around the maintenance target is used in the derivations of PMA rates reported in the Results, so we denote it by $H(t)$. For $t\ge\tau$:
\begin{equation}
y(t)=m+H(t), \qquad H(\tau)=0.
\label{eq:Hdef}
\end{equation}
By Equations~\eqref{eq:epsilon} and~\eqref{eq:y_delay}, $H(t)$ is the output of the first-order filter driven by the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process $\varepsilon(t)$. A PMA in the selected response channel occurs when these fluctuations carry the motor-preparatory signal across the gating level without subsequently producing sufficient neural drive to reach the response criterion.

\emph{Temporal preparation.} The two candidate models differ in whether the motor-preparatory signal reaching the downstream gate includes an additional contribution from temporal preparation. Model 1 includes no such contribution. Model 2 assumes that preparation for the selected response can additionally increase as the expected opportunity to act approaches. This assumption is motivated by evidence that learned or predictable temporal regularities can shape anticipatory motor preparation \parencites{heidemanTemporalAlignmentAnticipatory2018}{liBetaOscillationsInternal2026}{ramayyaHumanResponseTimes2025}. In the present task, RS onset occurs 3 s after motion-stimulus offset irrespective of stimulus duration, defining a fixed temporal interval between these two events. We therefore anchored temporal preparation to stimulus offset. We define $t_{\mathrm{offset}}$, the time at which stimulus offset becomes internally available as a temporal reference, as:
\[ t_{\mathrm{offset}}=D+L_{\mathrm{motion}}, \]
which coincides with the time at which encoded motion evidence ceases. To define the corresponding internal time of the RS, we introduced an RS-evidence encoding latency $L_{\mathrm{RS}}$, representing the delay between physical RS onset and the availability of RS-related evidence to the accumulation process. Accordingly, $t_{\mathrm{RS}}$, the time at which RS-related evidence becomes internally available to the accumulation process, is defined as:
\[ t_{\mathrm{RS}}=D+3+L_{\mathrm{RS}}. \]
Because maintenance begins no later than $t_{\mathrm{offset}}$, the selected response $s_{\mathrm{resp}}$ is always available when temporal preparation begins. During the interval from $t_{\mathrm{offset}}$ to $t_{\mathrm{RS}}$, Model 1 continues to transmit $y(t)$ to the downstream gate. In Model 2, temporal preparation instead provides an additional response-specific contribution that increases linearly from zero at $t_{\mathrm{offset}}$ to a maximum magnitude $\beta$ at $t_{\mathrm{RS}}$. Thus, during this interval, the signal reaching the gate, $\tilde{y}(t)$, is given by:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{y}(t):= y(t)+s_{\mathrm{resp}}\beta\,\frac{t-t_{\mathrm{offset}}}{t_{\mathrm{RS}}-t_{\mathrm{offset}}}, \qquad t_{\mathrm{offset}}\le t\le t_{\mathrm{RS}}.
\label{eq:tildey}
\end{equation}
The tilde distinguishes the signal reaching the gate from the maintained motor-preparatory state $y(t)$ itself. We assumed that the temporal-preparation contribution does not pass through the low-pass filter and is instead added directly to the signal reaching the downstream gate. The rationale is that the filter, inherited from the immediate-report architecture, describes the transformation of decision-related input $x(t)$ into the motor-preparatory state $y(t)$. Temporal preparation, by contrast, arises from learned temporal regularities and is treated as a distinct source of motor preparation rather than part of this decision-to-motor transformation. Accordingly, it alters neither the maintenance target $m$ nor the dynamics through which $y(t)$ is maintained.

\emph{RS-driven response initiation.} At $t_{\mathrm{RS}}$, once RS evidence becomes available to the accumulation process, the maintenance-noise process $\varepsilon(t)$ is discontinued, and the motor-preparatory state $y(t_{\mathrm{RS}})$ provides the baseline for subsequent RS-driven processing. In Model 2, the temporal-preparation contribution has simultaneously reached its maximum magnitude $s_{\mathrm{resp}}\beta$ and remains fixed thereafter. RS-driven processing is modeled by a distinct stochastic variable $q(t)$, which accumulates noisy evidence that the RS is present:
\begin{equation}
\dd q(t)=\mu_{\mathrm{RS}}\,\dd t+\sigma_{q}\,\dd W_{q}(t), \qquad q(t_{\mathrm{RS}})=0,
\label{eq:q}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_{\mathrm{RS}}$ is the mean rate of RS-evidence accumulation, $\sigma_{q}$ is the diffusion coefficient controlling within-trial noise, and $\dd W_{q}(t)$ is an increment of a standard Wiener process. As explained in the Introduction, noisy temporal integration of an unambiguous signal is consistent with accumulator accounts of one-choice RT \parencites{ratcliffDiffusionModelOnechoice2011}{smithPsychophysicallyPrincipledModels1995} and with EEG signatures of evidence accumulation under near-ceiling target-detection conditions \parencite{oconnellSupramodalAccumulationtoboundSignal2012}. The variable $q(t)$ has no independent boundary. Its accumulated value is continuously transmitted along the selected response direction and added to the input of the motor-preparation filter:
\begin{equation}
\dd y(t)=\lambda\left[y(t_{\mathrm{RS}})+s_{\mathrm{resp}}q(t)-y(t)\right]\dd t, \qquad t\ge t_{\mathrm{RS}}.
\label{eq:y_RS}
\end{equation}
The term $s_{\mathrm{resp}}q(t)$ therefore provides an increasing response-directed drive as RS evidence accumulates. In Model 2, the temporal-preparation contribution remains separate from this filtered input, consistent with its treatment during the delay. From $t_{\mathrm{RS}}$ onward, the signal reaching the gate is:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{y}(t) := \begin{cases} y(t), & \text{Model 1},\\ y(t)+s_{\mathrm{resp}}\beta, & \text{Model 2}.\end{cases}
\label{eq:tildey_RS}
\end{equation}
Once the corresponding neural drive reaches the response criterion $r$, the overt response occurs after the residual motor latency $L_{\mathrm{motor}}$, as in the immediate-report architecture.

Because gating inhibition remains operative throughout the delayed-report trial, fluctuations in motor preparation can transiently cross the gate during motion processing, maintenance, and RS-driven response initiation. If the resulting neural drive returns to zero before reaching $r$, the gate crossing generates a PMA rather than an overt response. The time-varying PMA rate therefore provides a nonlinear peripheral readout of the position and variability of the motor-preparatory signal relative to the gating and response criteria. The model also predicts the timing of response-related muscle activation and the subsequent overt response. Accordingly, we define \emph{premotor time} (PMT) as the interval from RS onset to the onset of the response-related muscle activation, and \emph{motor time} (MT) as the interval from muscle-activation onset to the overt response.

The models thus generate predictions for response accuracy, mean RT, mean PMT, mean MT, and PMA dynamics. For several aspects of these outcomes, analytical predictions can be derived, with detailed derivations provided in the Appendix. These derivations establish constraints on model predictions, some of which hold independently of parameter values, but do not fully characterize the effect magnitudes arising when parameters vary jointly. We therefore complemented them with simulations spanning a broad parameter space to quantify these magnitudes for the behavioral and EMG measures analyzed here and assess whether they fall within the empirical 95\% confidence intervals. Rather than estimating participant-specific parameters, this approach evaluates model predictions across different parameter combinations. Participant-level parameter estimation would require demonstrating reliable parameter recovery through a dedicated identifiability analysis. EEG was used as a complementary qualitative constraint on the temporal organization of motor preparation, because the present framework does not specify a forward observation model linking its internal variables to scalp potentials or spectral amplitude.


\subsection{Evaluation over the parameter space}

The model equations were integrated numerically in Python using NumPy and the explicit Euler--Maruyama scheme, with a fixed time step of 1/1024 s, corresponding to the sampling interval of the electrophysiological recordings described below. Each simulated trial yielded a trajectory of neural drive from stimulus onset until either the response or the end of the trial. A muscle activation began each time the neural drive became positive (Equation~\eqref{eq:z}). The last activation before the response was the response-related activation, and every other activation was a PMA. No further detection criterion was applied. Trials were simulated independently within each condition using a fixed pseudorandom seed to ensure reproducibility.

Table~\ref{tab:params} summarizes the model variables and parameters, together with their descriptions and, where applicable, the values or ranges used in the simulations. The sampled ranges were informed by the experimental design, structural constraints, and estimates from \textcite{dendauwGatedCascadeDiffusion2024} for parameters inherited from the immediate-report architecture. \textcite{dendauwGatedCascadeDiffusion2024} estimated these parameters by fitting the same decision-to-motor framework to a closely matched random-dot motion task in which responses were reported immediately. Because the present task closely matches theirs in its motion stimulus and response effectors, their estimates provide plausible reference values for the parameters inherited from the immediate-report architecture. However, the requirement to withhold the response may alter these parameter values. We therefore sampled $k$ and $\lambda$ over ranges extending roughly a factor of two on either side of the corresponding estimates of \textcite{dendauwGatedCascadeDiffusion2024}. The response criterion $r$ and the gating parameter $g$ were sampled over broader ranges, $g$ extending above the corresponding estimate, because withholding an already selected response likely requires stronger gating than an immediate-report task. By contrast, latency parameters ($L_{\mathrm{motion}}$, $L_{\mathrm{RS}}$, and $L_{\mathrm{motor}}$) were treated as fixed nuisance components of response timing because they reflect sensory-encoding or peripheral motor processes rather than the accumulation and maintenance mechanisms of primary interest. Because the model is invariant under a joint rescaling of its amplitude parameters, $\sigma_{x}$ was fixed at 0.1 to set the overall scale, an arbitrary normalization consistent with \textcite{dendauwGatedCascadeDiffusion2024}. The remaining amplitude parameters were therefore expressed relative to this reference scale. No previous study directly constrains $\kappa_{\varepsilon}$, which controls how rapidly maintenance-related deviations return toward zero. To avoid imposing a narrow timescale a priori, we sampled $\kappa_{\varepsilon}$ over a broad range, corresponding to characteristic decay times $1/\kappa_{\varepsilon}$ from 5 s, longer than the 3-s delay in our experiment, to approximately 33 ms. The internal threshold $\theta$ was sampled relative to the gating level through the ratio $\theta/g$, thereby enforcing the model constraint $\theta<g$. Maintenance-noise amplitude was parameterized by its stationary standard deviation, $\mathrm{SD}_{\varepsilon}$, with $\sigma_{\varepsilon}=\mathrm{SD}_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{2\kappa_{\varepsilon}}$, so that fluctuation magnitude could vary independently of $\kappa_{\varepsilon}$. The remaining delayed-report parameters governed temporal preparation and RS-driven response initiation. Because no previous delayed-report estimates were available, $\beta$, $\mu_{\mathrm{RS}}$, and $\sigma_{q}$ were sampled over broad ranges, with $\beta$ sampled only for Model 2.

Parameters were drawn using Latin-hypercube sampling over the ranges specified in Table~\ref{tab:params}. We generated 1,200 parameter vectors, each of which was evaluated in both models using the same parameter values, with $\beta$ set to zero in Model 1. For each vector, 40,000 trials were simulated per experimental condition and model. Admissibility was evaluated separately for each model. The purpose of this screening step was not to select parameterizations that reproduced the empirical condition effects, but to exclude parameter regimes producing implausible overall task performance or muscle activity. In every condition, the response-related muscle activation could begin before the RS on no more than 10\% of trials (this includes every response before the RS), responses could occur within 0.2 s after the RS on no more than 10\%, and failures to respond before the 4-s deadline on no more than 5\%. To exclude degenerate regimes of muscle activity without constraining the model to the present data, we additionally required the mean delay-window PMA rate to fall within a broad range of 0.02--5 s$^{-1}$. Of the 1,200 vectors, 228 were admissible in Model 1 and 204 in Model 2. For each admissible parameter vector, we retained response accuracy, RT, PMT, MT, and the onset times and response channels of PMAs, separately for each of the six motion coherence $\times$ duration conditions. These simulated outcomes formed the basis for the subsequent comparisons with empirical behavioral and EMG measures.

Figure~\ref{fig:dynamics} shows thirty parameter vectors admissible in both models, so that it displays the spread across the parameter space. Figure~\ref{fig:design}B shows a single simulated trial at one representative vector: among the vectors admissible in both models, the one closest to the coordinate-wise median of their parameters, each parameter standardized by its interquartile range and taken in logarithm where it was sampled in logarithm, with $\beta$ set to the median of its values over Model 2's admissible vectors. This rule involves the parameters only.

As a robustness analysis, we examined whether the models' predictions depended on the assumption of perfect integration in the two evidence-accumulation processes. We therefore repeated the parameter-space evaluation with leakage introduced into the motion-evidence accumulator $x(t)$ and into the RS-evidence accumulator $q(t)$, at rates $\gamma_{x}$ and $\gamma_{q}$ (Appendix, Remark~\ref{rem:leak}). The two rates were varied independently over $\{0, 0.25, 0.5, 1\}$ s$^{-1}$, which gives 15 combinations besides the main evaluation $\gamma_{x}=\gamma_{q}=0$; a rate of 0.25, 0.5, or 1 s$^{-1}$ corresponds to an integration time constant of 4, 2, or 1 s. Each combination was evaluated on the 304 vectors admissible in either model without leakage, with the same parameter values and the same number of trials.


\subsection{EMG acquisition and preprocessing}

EMG activity from the \emph{flexor pollicis brevis}, which flexes the thumb at the metacarpophalangeal joint and contributes to flexion at the carpometacarpal joint, was continuously recorded during task performance using two active Ag/AgCl electrodes placed 1 cm apart on the skin over the thenar eminence of each hand. The electrodes were connected to a BioSemi ActiveTwo Mk2 biopotential measurement system (sampling rate = 1024 Hz). After acquisition, the voltage difference between each electrode pair was computed. Left- and right-hand bipolar EMG signals were then high-pass filtered at 10 Hz using a third-order Butterworth filter to remove slow drifts unrelated to muscle activity, full-wave rectified, and segmented from $-$0.3 to 9 s relative to stimulus onset.

Muscle activation onsets and offsets were detected using the three-step semi-automatic procedure described in \textcite{achardExtendingContinuousFlow2025}. For each trial and EMG channel, the mean and standard deviation of the full-wave rectified signal were computed during the 300-ms prestimulus period. Candidate muscle-activity periods were identified when signal amplitude exceeded the mean by seven standard deviations. At this initial detection stage, threshold-defined periods separated by less than 25 ms were merged into a single candidate burst to prevent brief within-burst drops below the conservative detection threshold from artificially splitting a single activation. Candidate bursts were then filtered to limit false detections arising from background noise. Specifically, candidate bursts containing at most six above-threshold samples were counted across the epoch, and their occurrence rate per second was added to a base minimum of three above-threshold samples. Candidate bursts not meeting this adaptive criterion were discarded. The onset and offset of each retained candidate burst were then refined using the integrated-profile method \parencites{liuUseIntegratedProfile2016}{santelloControlTimingAmplitude1998}. For each burst, the integrated profile was computed from the cumulative rectified EMG in a window encompassing the burst and adjacent background activity, supplemented with additional background EMG from the beginning and end of the epoch. The overall linear trend of this cumulative signal was then removed; inclusion of the additional background activity reduced the influence of the burst itself on the trend estimate. The resulting profile was smoothed using a 15-ms moving average, and its minimum and subsequent maximum were taken as estimates of burst onset and offset, respectively. The search for these extrema extended up to 50 ms before the threshold-defined onset and 50 ms after the threshold-defined offset. For neighboring candidate bursts, the search ranges were truncated at the midpoint of the interval between the threshold-defined offset of the first burst and the threshold-defined onset of the next, preventing overlap. The integrated-profile method improved the temporal localization of burst boundaries relative to the initial high-threshold detection, yielding more precise estimates of onset and offset. Finally, every trial was visually inspected and erroneous onset markers were manually corrected. Trials for which excessive noise prevented reliable onset detection were excluded from subsequent analyses. For each retained trial, the response-related muscle activation was identified as the final EMG burst in the responding hand preceding the button press. Its onset was used to partition RT into PMT and MT, as defined in the theoretical framework. All other detected bursts beginning between stimulus onset and the button press were classified as PMAs. Together with response accuracy and RT, these measures provided the empirical counterparts of the simulated outcomes.


\subsection{EEG acquisition and preprocessing}

EEG signals were recorded continuously during task performance from 64 active Ag/AgCl electrodes positioned according to the international 10--20 system, using the same BioSemi ActiveTwo system and sampling rate as the EMG recordings. Vertical and horizontal eye movements were recorded using electro-oculogram electrodes: two placed above and below the right eye to measure vertical movements, and two placed at the outer canthi of the eyes to measure horizontal movements. The data were preprocessed using custom scripts together with functions from the MNE-Python toolbox (v1.9.0). Horizontal and vertical electro-oculogram signals were derived by computing the voltage difference between the corresponding electrode pairs. EEG channels exhibiting excessive variance or drifts were interpolated using the spherical spline method, and the data were re-referenced to the average of all electrodes. Ocular artifacts were corrected using independent component analysis. For independent component analysis decomposition only, the continuous EEG data were high-pass filtered at 1 Hz to remove slow drifts and low-frequency noise that could impair artifact isolation, and then epoched from $-$0.6 to 9 s relative to stimulus onset. Independent component analysis was implemented using the default parameters of MNE's independent component analysis function. Components associated with eye movements were identified using MNE's \emph{find\_bads\_eog} function, which detects electro-oculogram-related components based on a z-score-transformed Pearson correlation between independent component analysis components and electro-oculogram signals filtered in the 1--10 Hz range. Components with z-scores exceeding a threshold of 3 were masked at each iteration, and z-scores were recalculated on the remaining components. This iterative procedure enables adaptive identification of ocular artifacts and, together with visual inspection, ensured robust detection of eye-movement-related components. Up to four components were removed per participant. The resulting independent component analysis weights were then applied to EEG data that had been filtered between 0.1 and 40 Hz before segmentation. All EEG and electro-oculogram preprocessing filters were finite impulse response filters with a Hamming window. The epoched data were baseline-corrected using the 0.1 s window preceding stimulus onset, and epochs were excluded from further analysis if any scalp channel exceeded an absolute amplitude of 150 $\mu$V (\emph{M} = 10.1\%, range 1.35--21.98\%). Finally, the EEG data were transformed to current source density using the corresponding MNE function with default parameters, to reduce the spatial blurring effects of volume conduction \parencites[e.g.,][]{kellyNeurocomputationalMechanismsPriorinformed2021}{kellyInternalExternalInfluences2013}{twomeyAbstractEffectorSelectiveDecision2016}.

To characterize the cortical dynamics of motor preparation, we analyzed two established EEG markers: mu--beta-band spectral amplitude lateralization (MBL), a time-frequency measure capturing asymmetric suppression of mu--beta oscillations over motor cortices, and the lateralized readiness potential (LRP), a time-domain event-related potential. Both markers are computed by contrasting activity over motor areas contralateral and ipsilateral to the responding effector. MBL reflects the relative decrease in mu--beta amplitude (8--30 Hz) over the contralateral hemisphere during motor preparation \parencite{pfurtschellerEventrelatedEEGMEG1999}. The LRP is a negative-going potential associated with effector-specific motor activation, typically interpreted as reflecting a later stage of response preparation \parencites{eimerLateralizedReadinessPotential1998}{grattonPrePoststimulusActivation1988}. In immediate-report perceptual decisions, both signals exhibit evidence-dependent buildup and have often been treated as interchangeable indices of motor preparation \parencites{kellyInternalExternalInfluences2013}{kellyNeurocomputationalMechanismsPriorinformed2021}. However, delayed-report studies suggest a functional dissociation: MBL can develop during evidence accumulation and persist while responding is withheld, whereas the LRP may emerge predominantly after the response signal \parencite{roggeMotorCorticalSignals2022}. In the absence of a forward model linking the models' internal variables to EEG signals, we used these markers as complementary qualitative constraints on the proposed temporal organization of motor preparation.

To measure MBL, we extracted mu--beta amplitude (8--30 Hz) over time at each electrode using a short-time Fourier transform of the current source density signals, with a 0.3-s window and a 0.1-s step. To identify electrodes exhibiting maximal effector-selective activity, we computed a grand-average topography for the $-$0.2 to 0 s interval preceding the response. At each electrode, mean mu--beta amplitude for right-hand trials was subtracted from that for left-hand trials, collapsing across experimental conditions. An analogous topography was constructed using time-domain current source density amplitudes. Both topographies revealed extrema at C3 and C4 (Fig.~\ref{fig:pmaeeg}C,D), which were therefore selected for subsequent analyses. For each trial, MBL and the LRP were then computed by subtracting the signal ipsilateral to the responding hand from the contralateral signal, using mu--beta amplitude and time-domain current source density, respectively.


\subsection{Statistical analysis}

All analyses included both correct and incorrect trials that passed the behavioral, EMG, and EEG exclusion criteria. To characterize the temporal evolution of PMAs, MBL, and the LRP, we used a common temporal grid of 0.1-s bins. For PMAs, this bin width was chosen as a compromise between temporal resolution and the stability of event-count estimates. Each activation was assigned to a bin according to its onset. For each participant, coherence $\times$ duration condition, and EMG channel, the mean number of activations per trial in each 0.1-s bin was divided by bin width to obtain a mean activation rate. The same binning and normalization were applied to the simulated data. Channels were classified as response-associated or opposite relative to the executed response, irrespective of response accuracy. For MBL, the temporal grid matched the spacing of successive spectral estimates, whereas LRP amplitude was averaged within each bin.

We examined four event-locked analysis windows. The stimulus-onset window covered the first 2 s after motion onset (20 bins), including the early part of the delay in the 0.3-s duration condition. The delay window covered the interval from 1 to 3 s after motion offset (20 bins), excluding the first second to minimize contributions from evidence accumulation and overlap with the stimulus-onset analysis. Given the relatively short RTs expected, the RS and response windows each spanned 0.2 s (2 bins), immediately after signal onset and before the button press, respectively. The RS window matched the RT exclusion threshold, ensuring that all retained trials contributed to both bins, regardless of RT.

For the stimulus-onset and delay windows, we characterized the temporal profiles of PMAs (separately for each EMG channel), MBL amplitude, and LRP amplitude. Each profile was summarized by its mean level across bins, its linear slope, and its quadratic curvature, computed separately for each participant and coherence $\times$ duration condition. Linear slopes were estimated by fitting a straight line to the successive bin values using least squares, quantifying the direction and rate of change over time. Quadratic curvature was estimated by fitting a second-order polynomial to characterize departures from linearity, including accelerating or decelerating trajectories. Fits used bin-center times expressed in seconds and centered within each analysis window. Quadratic curvature denotes the coefficient of the squared time term. For PMAs, mean rate, linear slope, and quadratic curvature were expressed in s$^{-1}$, s$^{-2}$, and s$^{-3}$, respectively. This procedure yielded one estimate of each temporal characteristic per participant and experimental condition, which was subsequently used as a dependent variable in the statistical analyses.

The RS and response windows were treated differently. Given their limited interpretability over these short intervals, PMAs and MBL were examined descriptively rather than inferentially. Specifically, by definition, no PMA can occur in the response-associated EMG channel after the onset of the response-related EMG burst, making a decline in their observed occurrence expected as the response approaches. For MBL, substantial overlap between successive spectral estimates limits the temporal resolution available within these short windows. In contrast, the higher temporal resolution of the LRP allowed meaningful inferential analyses of its mean level and linear slope across the two bins.

Statistical inference relied on repeated-measures ANOVAs, with a significance threshold of $\alpha$ = .05. Response accuracy, mean RT, mean PMT, and mean MT were each analyzed using motion coherence (0\%, 12\%, and 50\%) and duration (0.3 and 2 s) as within-participant factors. For the stimulus-onset and delay windows, the participant-level estimates of mean level, linear slope, and quadratic curvature were analyzed using separate coherence $\times$ duration repeated-measures ANOVAs. For the LRP in the RS and response windows, analyses were restricted to the mean level and the linear slope, as the two-bin windows did not allow estimation of quadratic curvature. Overall linear and quadratic temporal trends were also tested against zero, averaging across coherence and duration conditions. Sphericity was assessed using Mauchly's test, and Greenhouse--Geisser corrections were applied where appropriate. Analyses were conducted in R using the afex and emmeans packages. Full results are given in Tables~\ref{tab:behav}--\ref{tab:mbl}.


\subsection{Model--data comparison}

We compared individual behavioral and EMG effects with predictions generated by each model across its sampled admissible parameter vectors. Quantitative comparisons focused on effects informative about the models' theoretical predictions: the twenty effects of Table~\ref{tab:comparison}, on accuracy, RT, PMT, MT, and the PMA rate in the stimulus-onset and delay windows. For each effect, a signed estimate was calculated separately for each participant and then averaged across participants, with a 95\% confidence interval for the group mean calculated using Student's \emph{t} distribution. The same effect was calculated from the simulated data for each admissible parameter vector, using the same definitions and procedures as for the empirical data. A vector entered the comparison for an effect only if all six conditions yielded the quantities the effect is computed from; this excluded 2 of Model 1's 228 vectors from the three effects on the delay-window slope and curvature, in which one condition produced too few PMAs. Monte Carlo standard errors were estimated from the variability across simulated trials. Given the large number of simulated trials per condition, the corresponding 95\% confidence intervals were calculated using the normal approximation (estimate $\pm$ 1.96 Monte Carlo standard errors). A parameter vector was classified as reproducing the empirical magnitude when its entire Monte Carlo interval lay within the empirical 95\% confidence interval, as not reproducing it when the intervals were disjoint, and as numerically uncertain otherwise. A model was considered able to reproduce an effect's magnitude if at least one sampled admissible vector met the first criterion. The magnitude was considered unreproduced within the explored parameter space only when all sampled admissible vectors met the second criterion. For each model and effect, we report the sampled minimum and maximum effect estimates and the number and percentage of vectors in each magnitude category. For effects whose direction was not fixed by the analytical derivations across parameter values, we additionally summarized the simulated effect signs. An effect was classified as having the observed sign or the opposite sign when its Monte Carlo confidence interval excluded zero; otherwise, its sign was classified as uncertain. We report the number and percentage of vectors in each sign category for these effects.


\section{Results}

The first subsection concerns accuracy, RT, PMT and MT. Each of the following subsections takes one analysis window. Each states what the two models predict, with the longer derivations in the Appendix, and tests the prediction against the data. For accuracy, RT, PMT, MT, and the PMA rate in the stimulus-onset and delay windows, it then compares the observed effect magnitudes with those the models produce over their admissible parameter vectors (Materials and Methods; Table~\ref{tab:comparison} and Fig.~\ref{fig:effects}).


\subsection{Accuracy, RT, PMT and MT}


\subsubsection{Theoretical predictions}

The two models differ in one term, the temporal preparation of magnitude $\beta$, which enters through $\delta:=t_{\mathrm{RS}}-t_{\mathrm{offset}}=3+L_{\mathrm{RS}}-L_{\mathrm{motion}}$. Stimulus duration cancels from $\delta$, so temporal preparation adds the same amount in all six coherence $\times$ duration conditions and can generate no effect of coherence, of duration, or of their interaction. Every prediction below therefore holds in both models, with the same sign.

The choice is the sign of $x$ when accumulation stops. By Equation~\eqref{eq:x} its drift is $skc$, so accuracy rises with coherence and with the time for which Equation~\eqref{eq:x} runs, hence with duration. The duration benefit is confined to intermediate coherence, which is a coherence $\times$ duration interaction on accuracy: at $c=0$ the drift vanishes and accuracy is 50\% whatever the duration, and at the highest coherence the threshold $\theta$ is typically reached within the short stimulus, so accumulation stops before the extra time can be used.

After the RS, the second accumulation carries the signal from the maintenance target $m$ to the response criterion, at the drift $\mu_{\mathrm{RS}}$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:q}, which does not depend on the condition. Coherence and duration therefore act on RT only through $m$ (Appendix, Remark~\ref{rem:leak}). By Equation~\eqref{eq:m}, $m$ is $s_{\mathrm{resp}}\theta$ on trials that reached the threshold and $y(L_{\mathrm{motion}}+D)$ on the others; both branches rise with coherence and the second rises with duration, so mean RT falls with either. The first branch is a cap, and it produces a coherence $\times$ duration interaction on RT: the coherence effect is larger after the 0.3-s stimulus than after the 2-s one. After a long stimulus most trials sit at $\theta$ at every coherence, so $m$ barely varies with coherence, whereas after a short one fewer trials reach $\theta$ and $m$ still does.

These effects are predicted to be carried by PMT, which covers the rise of the signal from $m$ to the gate. MT covers the rise from the gate to the criterion, whose height $r$ is the same in every condition. It depends on the condition only through the speed of the signal when it crosses the gate, so its effects are predicted to be small, with no sign fixed by the derivation.


\subsubsection{Empirical results}

Figures~\ref{fig:behavior}A and~\ref{fig:behavior}B show response accuracy and mean RT as a function of motion coherence and duration; full ANOVA results are in Table~\ref{tab:behav}. Accuracy decreased as motion coherence decreased (F(1.30,27.40) = 222.22, p < .001, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.914) and was higher in the 2-s condition than in the 0.3-s condition (F(1,21) = 22.68, p < .001, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.519). This duration benefit was observed only at the intermediate 12\% coherence level, yielding a significant motion coherence $\times$ duration interaction (F(1.96,41.24) = 12.17, p < .001, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.367). Mean RT increased as motion coherence and duration decreased (coherence: F(1.49,31.39) = 14.19, p < .001, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.403; duration: F(1,21) = 65.79, p < .001, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.758), with the effect of coherence more pronounced in the 0.3-s condition (coherence $\times$ duration: F(1.68,35.25) = 4.61, p = .022, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.180).

Mean PMT and mean MT are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:behavior}C and~\ref{fig:behavior}D. Mean PMT showed the same pattern of effects as mean RT (duration: F(1,21) = 69.60, p < .001, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.768; coherence: F(1.60,33.50) = 14.00, p < .001, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.400; duration $\times$ coherence: F(1.72,36.14) = 3.58, p = .045, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.145), with a larger coherence effect in the 0.3-s condition. Mean MT was not significantly affected by any factor (duration: F(1,21) = 1.10, p = .307, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.050; coherence: F(1.51,31.73) = 0.62, p = .499, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.029; duration $\times$ coherence: F(1.86,39.12) = 2.44, p = .104, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.104). The effects of coherence and duration on RT are therefore confined to PMT, as both models predict.


\subsubsection{Model--data comparison}

Ten effects were compared (Table~\ref{tab:comparison}; Fig.~\ref{fig:effects}A--J). Each is reproduced by both models at some admissible vectors, and the two models do not differ on any of them.

On accuracy, the coherence effect (50\% minus 0\%; observed 0.447 [0.419, 0.475]) is reproduced by 40 of Model 1's 228 admissible vectors and 29 of Model 2's 204, and the duration effect (0.031 [0.017, 0.044]) by 83 and 64. The interaction, measured as the duration benefit at 12\% coherence minus the mean duration benefit at 0\% and 50\% (0.075 [0.047, 0.103]), is reproduced by 45 and 40 vectors; its sign is the observed one at 223 and 199 vectors and the opposite one at none.

On RT, the coherence effect (0\% minus 50\%; 32.5 ms [19.6, 45.5]) is reproduced by 51 and 41 vectors, and the duration effect (0.3 s minus 2 s; 48.5 ms [36.0, 61.0]) by 31 and 40. The interaction, measured as the coherence effect after the 0.3-s stimulus minus the coherence effect after the 2-s stimulus (14.5 ms [$-$2.1, 31.1]), is reproduced by 129 and 112 vectors; its sign is the observed one at 217 and 193 vectors and the opposite one at 4 and 5. The same three effects on PMT (31.7 ms [19.0, 44.4], 49.0 ms [36.4, 61.6], and 11.2 ms [$-$5.4, 27.8]) are reproduced by 63 and 54, 32 and 39, and 86 and 75 vectors; the interaction has the observed sign at 214 and 191 vectors and the opposite sign at 5 in each model.

The duration effect on MT ($-$0.5 ms [$-$2.1, 1.1]) is reproduced by 63 and 51 vectors. At most other vectors the models make MT shorter after the 0.3-s stimulus than after the 2-s one, by up to 82 ms (Model 1) and 90 ms (Model 2); this sign is that of the observed estimate at 213 and 194 vectors, and the opposite one at none and 1. The models thus produce the dissociation between PMT and MT, but at many parameter vectors not exactly: the passage from the gate to the criterion is then faster after the short stimulus.


\subsection{Stimulus-onset window}


\subsubsection{Theoretical predictions}

By Equation~\eqref{eq:x} the drift of the first accumulation is $skc$, so a higher coherence carries $x$, and through Equation~\eqref{eq:y} the motor-preparatory signal $y$, further and faster. A PMA requires that signal to exceed the gate $g$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:z}), so the mean PMA rate over the window is predicted to be higher at higher coherence. Neither Equation~\eqref{eq:x} nor Equation~\eqref{eq:y} involves $\beta$, so this prediction is common to both models. The derivation fixes no sign for the effect of coherence on the slope of the rate within the window.

Duration acts differently in the two models. A trial that is still accumulating cannot produce a PMA: accumulation stops when $|y|$ reaches $\theta$, and $\theta<g$ (Appendix, Proposition~\ref{prop:duration}). After the 2-s stimulus, more trials reach $\theta$, the maintenance target closest to the gate, while the window is under way. After the 0.3-s stimulus, the trials that have not reached $\theta$ are maintained below it, but from the end of the stimulus onward. Under Model 1 the first mechanism raises the rate after the long stimulus and the second raises it after the short one; the derivation does not fix which dominates (Appendix, Proposition~\ref{prop:duration}), and the answer is numerical (Model--data comparison, below). Under Model 2, temporal preparation begins at stimulus offset, and therefore inside this window only after the 0.3-s stimulus; it raises the rate after the short stimulus. Whether it reverses the sign of the duration effect depends on $\beta$ relative to the distance from the maintenance target to the gate, two independent parameters.


\subsubsection{Empirical results}

\paragraph{PMA rate}

Figure~\ref{fig:pmaeeg}A shows the PMA rate in the response-associated EMG channel and Figure~\ref{fig:pmaeeg}B the rate in the opposite channel, in each analysis window. PMA rates in the opposite channel were near zero across conditions and windows, so the analyses below concern the response-associated channel (full results in Table~\ref{tab:pma}). In the stimulus-onset window, the mean PMA rate was higher at higher coherence (F(1.33,28.01) = 5.25, p = .021, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.200), as both models predict. The rate increased over the window (slope 0.097 s$^{-2}$ [0.060, 0.133], F(1,21) = 30.87, p < .001, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.595), and this slope was steeper at higher coherence (F(1.72,36.20) = 6.15, p = .007, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.226), consistent with evidence accumulation. The mean rate was higher after the 0.3-s stimulus than after the 2-s stimulus (F(1,21) = 13.38, p = .001, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.389), and the slope was steeper (F(1,21) = 9.53, p = .006, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.312). This duration effect is unlikely to reflect a greater allocation of attention in the 0.3-s condition, because stimulus duration was unpredictable at trial onset.

\paragraph{MBL}

Figure~\ref{fig:pmaeeg}D shows MBL amplitude in each condition and window (full results in Table~\ref{tab:mbl}). In the stimulus-onset window, MBL rose (slope: F(1,21) = 11.74, p = .003, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.359) and bent (curvature: F(1,21) = 9.12, p = .007, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.303): it rose over approximately the first second and then flattened. The effect of coherence on the slope over the whole window was not significant (F(1.95,40.99) = 0.52, p = .596, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.024). Fitted separately in the two halves of the window, the slope depended on coherence in the first second (F = 5.76, p = .018) and not in the second (F = 0.37, p = .658; Table~\ref{tab:split}). The coherence-dependent trajectories thus converged toward a common sustained level, consistent with accumulation toward an internal threshold.

\paragraph{LRP}

Figure~\ref{fig:pmaeeg}C shows the LRP in each condition and window (full results in Table~\ref{tab:lrp}). In the stimulus-onset window, the LRP did not change significantly over time (slope: F(1,21) = 0.50, p = .489), and coherence affected neither its level (F(1.75,36.66) = 1.50, p = .237) nor its slope (F(1.49,31.27) = 0.55, p = .531). Only the effect of duration on the curvature was significant (F(1,21) = 7.20, p = .014, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.255).


\subsubsection{Model--data comparison}

Four effects were compared (Table~\ref{tab:comparison}; Fig.~\ref{fig:effects}K--N). The coherence effect on the mean rate (50\% minus 0\%; observed 0.054 s$^{-1}$ [0.012, 0.095]) is reproduced by 118 of Model 1's admissible vectors and 80 of Model 2's. The coherence effect on the slope (0.056 s$^{-2}$ [0.017, 0.096]) is reproduced by 36 and 49 vectors; its sign is the observed one at 128 and 149 vectors and the opposite one at 65 and 22.

The two duration effects separate the models. The duration effect on the mean rate (0.3 s minus 2 s; 0.057 s$^{-1}$ [0.026, 0.088]) is reproduced by no Model 1 vector: Model 1's values range from $-$0.986 to $-$0.001 s$^{-1}$, the opposite sign at 227 of its 228 vectors and an uncertain sign at the remaining one. Model 2's values range from $-$0.945 to 0.157 s$^{-1}$; 10 of its vectors reproduce the observed magnitude, and the sign is the observed one at 76 vectors and the opposite one at 109. The duration effect on the slope (0.060 s$^{-2}$ [0.020, 0.100]) follows the same pattern: no Model 1 vector reproduces it (values from $-$1.18 to $-$0.001 s$^{-2}$; opposite sign at 222 vectors, observed sign at none), whereas 21 Model 2 vectors do (values from $-$1.12 to 0.284 s$^{-2}$; observed sign at 85 vectors, opposite at 102). Temporal preparation thus opposes the effect of the longer accumulation, as derived above, and overturns it at part of Model 2's admissible space. Within the explored space, the observed duration effects are incompatible with Model 1 and compatible with Model 2.


\subsection{Delay window}


\subsubsection{Theoretical predictions}

During the delay, a PMA in the response-associated channel is an upcrossing, by the fluctuation $H$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:Hdef}, of the distance from the maintenance target to the gate. The Appendix derives the rate of those upcrossings in closed form. Two facts make the result simple here. First, the fluctuation of the signal about the maintenance target settles at a rate of at least $\lambda$, the gain of the filter that produces it, and has settled before the analysis window opens, at least 0.8 s after maintenance onset (Appendix, Proposition~\ref{prop:stationary}). Second, once it has settled, the rate depends on time only through the distance from the maintenance target to the gate. Writing $\sigma_{H}$ and $\sigma_{\dot{H}}$ for the standard deviations of the settled fluctuation and of its time derivative, $\omega:=\sqrt{\lambda\kappa_{\varepsilon}}$ for its characteristic frequency, and
\begin{equation}
a(t):=\frac{g-|m|-\dfrac{\beta}{\delta}\left(t-t_{\mathrm{offset}}\right)^{+}}{\sigma_{H}}
\label{eq:a}
\end{equation}
for the distance from the maintenance target to the gate in units of $\sigma_{H}$, which temporal preparation shortens at the constant speed $\beta/\delta$, the rate on the window is its stationary form $\nustat$, Equation~\eqref{eq:nu_asymp}. Over the admissible parameter vectors, the two differ by at most $3\times10^{-3}$ in relative terms under Model 1 and $1.2\times10^{-3}$ under Model 2 (Appendix, Corollary~\ref{cor:approx}; Fig.~\ref{fig:approx}):
\begin{equation}
\nustat(t)=\varphi\left(a(t)\right)\left[\frac{\beta}{\delta\sigma_{H}}\Phi\left(\frac{\beta}{\delta\sigma_{\dot{H}}}\right)+\omega\,\varphi\left(\frac{\beta}{\delta\sigma_{\dot{H}}}\right)\right],
\tag{\ref{eq:nu_asymp}}
\end{equation}
where $\varphi$ and $\Phi$ are the standard normal density and distribution function. Setting $\beta=0$ (Model 1) makes $a(t)$ constant, equal to $a=(g-|m|)/\sigma_{H}$, and recovers the classical Rice formula, in which the rate falls as a Gaussian in the standardized distance to the gate:
\begin{equation}
\nustat=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}e^{-a^{2}/2}.
\tag{\ref{eq:nu1}}
\end{equation}
Three properties of the delay-window rate follow: whether it ramps over the window, the shape of that ramp, and how its level differs across conditions.

\paragraph{The slope}

Under Model 1 the numerator of $a$ is the constant $g-|m|$ and the denominator is constant as well, so by Equation~\eqref{eq:nu1} the rate does not depend on time. Model 1 predicts a slope of zero, at every value of $\kappa_{\varepsilon}$, of $\lambda$, and of the maintenance target (Appendix, Corollary~\ref{cor:flat}). Under Model 2 the numerator falls at the constant speed $\beta/\delta$, so the rate rises throughout the delay. Taking logarithms in Equation~\eqref{eq:nu_asymp} gives that rise in closed form (Appendix, Corollary~\ref{cor:logslope}):
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd\left(\ln\nustat\right)}{\dd t}(t)=\frac{\beta}{\delta\sigma_{H}}\,a(t), \qquad \frac{\dd^{2}\left(\ln\nustat\right)}{\dd t^{2}}(t)=-\left(\frac{\beta}{\delta\sigma_{H}}\right)^{2}.
\tag{\ref{eq:slope}}
\end{equation}
We test this on the rate itself. Its rise,
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\dd\nustat}{\dd t}(t)=\nustat(t)\,\frac{\beta}{\delta\sigma_{H}}\,a(t),
\end{equation*}
is the product of two quantities: how often PMAs occur, and how fast temporal preparation closes the distance to the gate. A model must produce both to produce the observed slope. Averaged over the trials of a condition, the slope of the log rate is proportional to the mean standardized distance to the gate, each trial weighted by its own rate (Appendix, Corollary~\ref{cor:logslope}).

\paragraph{The curvature}

Under Model 1 the rate is constant, so its curvature is zero at every parameter value. Under Model 2, the mean rate of a condition is convex wherever the rate-weighted mean of $a(t)^{2}$ exceeds 1 (Appendix, Corollary~\ref{cor:logslope}), in particular wherever every contributing trial stands more than one fluctuation standard deviation from the gate. A positive curvature is therefore excluded by Model 1 and produced by Model 2 over that part of its parameter space.

\paragraph{The coherence $\times$ duration interaction}

How the level of the rate differs across conditions follows from Equation~\eqref{eq:m}. On a trial that reached the internal threshold, the maintenance target is $m=s_{\mathrm{resp}}\theta$ whatever the coherence; the distance to the gate is then $g-\theta$ in all three coherence conditions, and the law of $H$ does not depend on coherence either, so the rate is the same in all three. Coherence can act only through the trials that did not reach $\theta$, on which $m=y(L_{\mathrm{motion}}+D)$ grows with it, and a longer stimulus leaves fewer such trials. The coherence effect on the delay-window rate is therefore predicted to be smaller after the 2-s stimulus than after the 0.3-s one. The prediction holds where the 0\%-coherence, 2-s condition has itself mostly reached $\theta$, since a difference shrinks only once both of its terms have stopped growing. Where that fails it reverses, because below $\theta$ the maintenance target still grows with coherence, and grows more the longer the stimulus. The derivation therefore fixes no sign for this interaction.

The two EEG measures index the same maintained state without being derived from the model, so the prediction for them is directional only: flat across the delay under Model 1, ramping under Model 2.


\subsubsection{Empirical results}

\paragraph{PMA rate}

Figure~\ref{fig:pmaeeg}A shows that the PMA rate continued to increase during the delay. Its slope across the delay window was 0.560 s$^{-2}$ [0.364, 0.757] (F(1,21) = 35.24, p < .001, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.627; Table~\ref{tab:pma}). This increase is best attributed to temporal preparation rather than to continued evidence accumulation: the effect of coherence on the slope, the marker of accumulation, was not significant in this window (F(1.70,35.61) = 0.52, p = .567, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.024), nor was its interaction with duration (F(1.74,36.45) = 0.51, p = .580, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.024). Model 1 cannot produce this slope at any parameter value (Theoretical predictions, above).

The rate did not merely rise; it accelerated. Its curvature was 0.210 s$^{-3}$ [0.109, 0.310] (F(1,21) = 18.89, p < .001, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.474), the sign Model 1 excludes. Neither duration (F(1,21) = 0.75, p = .396) nor coherence (F(1.95,40.89) = 1.86, p = .170) modulated the curvature.

The mean rate was higher after the 2-s than after the 0.3-s stimulus (F(1,21) = 33.19, p < .001, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.612), and this difference increased over the window (duration effect on the slope: F(1,21) = 11.90, p = .002, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.362). This is the reverse of the ordering seen in the stimulus-onset window, consistent with the different temporal alignment of the two windows. The mean rate was higher at higher coherence (F(1.96,41.15) = 3.36, p = .045, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.138), and a duration $\times$ coherence interaction (F(1.92,40.42) = 3.47, p = .042, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.142) showed a larger coherence effect after the 2-s than after the 0.3-s stimulus. This is the direction the derivation allows only where the 0\%-coherence, 2-s condition has mostly not reached $\theta$ (Theoretical predictions, above), and the opposite of the coherence $\times$ duration interaction on mean PMT. Selective exclusion of the most prepared trials through responses before the RS is an unlikely explanation, because such responses were rare (Fig.~\ref{fig:premature}).

\paragraph{MBL}

During the delay, MBL showed a slight decrease before settling into a sustained level, at least in the highest coherence conditions. This sustained level increased over the window (slope: F(1,21) = 7.11, p = .014, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.253; Table~\ref{tab:mbl}), suggesting that MBL also reflects temporal preparation. Neither coherence (F(1.29,27.12) = 1.26, p = .285) nor duration (F(1,21) = 3.18, p = .089) affected its level. This is the delay-window ramp Model 2 predicts and Model 1 does not.

\paragraph{LRP}

During the delay, the LRP became progressively more negative (slope: F(1,21) = 14.34, p = .001, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.406; Table~\ref{tab:lrp}), consistent with temporal preparation for the upcoming response. As for MBL, this is the ramp Model 2 predicts and Model 1 does not. Although the LRP primarily reflects the final stage of motor preparation, it thus also indexes temporal preparation for the selected response during the delay, suggesting that the underlying circuits are informed about the outcome of motion-evidence accumulation.


\subsubsection{Model--data comparison}

Six effects were compared (Table~\ref{tab:comparison}; Fig.~\ref{fig:effects}O--T).

\paragraph{The slope}

The slope of Model 1's rate is zero (Corollary~\ref{cor:flat}). Over its 226 admissible vectors with a measurable slope, the simulated slope ranges from $-$0.090 to 0.007 s$^{-2}$, and no vector reproduces the observed 0.560 s$^{-2}$ [0.364, 0.757]. The negative values come from the measurement, not from the model: the activation that becomes the response is excluded from the PMA count wherever it begins, and in the model it occasionally begins inside the last bins of the window (Appendix, Remark~\ref{rem:scope}). Over Model 2's 204 vectors the slope ranges from $-$0.029 to 1.064 s$^{-2}$, and 7 vectors reproduce the observed value. Figure~\ref{fig:shape} shows the profiles: every admissible vector of Model 1 is flat, whereas Model 2's fan out, many of them tracking the observed rise.

The duration effect on the slope (2 s minus 0.3 s; 0.135 s$^{-2}$ [0.054, 0.217]) separates the models in the same way. No Model 1 vector reproduces it (values from $-$0.057 to 0.006 s$^{-2}$; observed sign at 1 vector, opposite sign at 92, uncertain at 133), whereas 37 Model 2 vectors do (values from $-$0.041 to 0.847 s$^{-2}$; observed sign at 154, opposite at 21, uncertain at 29).

\paragraph{The curvature}

No Model 1 vector reproduces the observed curvature, 0.210 s$^{-3}$ [0.110, 0.310]: its values range from $-$0.136 to 0.005 s$^{-3}$ (observed sign at 1 vector, opposite at 142, uncertain at 83). Model 2's range from $-$0.190 to 0.660 s$^{-3}$, and 12 vectors reproduce the observed value (observed sign at 80, opposite at 78, uncertain at 46). The slope and the curvature are set by the same quantities: the speed at which temporal preparation closes the distance to the gate, and the rate-weighted mean and spread of that distance across trials (Appendix, Corollary~\ref{cor:logslope}).

\paragraph{The level}

The coherence and duration effects on the mean rate separate nothing, as the derivation says they cannot. The coherence effect (50\% minus 0\%; 0.093 s$^{-1}$ [0.013, 0.173]) is reproduced by 141 Model 1 vectors and 113 Model 2 vectors, and the duration effect (2 s minus 0.3 s; 0.312 s$^{-1}$ [0.198, 0.426]) by 24 and 28. The coherence $\times$ duration interaction, measured as the coherence effect after the 2-s stimulus minus the coherence effect after the 0.3-s stimulus (0.107 s$^{-1}$ [0.001, 0.213]), is reproduced by 10 vectors in each model. Its sign is, however, the opposite of the observed one at most vectors (206 of 228 in Model 1, 188 of 204 in Model 2) and the observed one at 11 and 12.

\paragraph{Leakage}

Leakage in either accumulator leaves every delay-window statement of the Appendix unchanged: it can move the level of Model 1's delay-window rate but cannot give it a time dependence (Appendix, Remark~\ref{rem:leak}). Over the full grid of leakage rates, the number of admissible vectors ranges from 218 to 228 in Model 1 and from 194 to 204 in Model 2, and no combination changes whether a model reproduces each effect of Table~\ref{tab:comparison}.


\subsection{RS window}


\subsubsection{Theoretical predictions}

After the RS, the second accumulation carries the signal toward the response criterion, so a gate crossing is more likely to continue on to the response than to fall back below the gate, and trials are progressively absorbed by the response. The PMA rate is therefore predicted to fall after the RS. The closed-form rate of the Appendix does not apply in this window: the criterion acts as an absorbing barrier, and conditioning on survival removes the Gaussianity on which Equation~\eqref{eq:nu_asymp} depends.


\subsubsection{Empirical results}

\paragraph{PMA rate}

As predicted, the PMA rate decreased after RS onset (Fig.~\ref{fig:pmaeeg}A): from 1.26 s$^{-1}$ in the first bin to 0.98 s$^{-1}$ in the second after the 0.3-s stimulus, and from 1.74 to 1.32 s$^{-1}$ after the 2-s stimulus.

\paragraph{MBL}

After RS onset, MBL amplitude did not show any clear modulation (Fig.~\ref{fig:pmaeeg}D).

\paragraph{LRP}

The LRP continued to become more negative after the RS (slope: F(1,21) = 14.08, p = .001, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.401; Table~\ref{tab:lrp}). Neither coherence nor duration affected its level or its slope (all p > .27).


\subsection{Response window}


\subsubsection{Theoretical predictions}

The account of the PMA decline after the RS predicts that the decline continues into the response window. In addition, no PMA can begin after the onset of the response-related activation, which on average precedes the button press by about 160 ms.


\subsubsection{Empirical results}

\paragraph{PMA rate}

The PMA rate was 0.21 s$^{-1}$ after the 0.3-s stimulus and 0.29 s$^{-1}$ after the 2-s stimulus in the bin from 0.2 to 0.1 s before the button press, and exactly zero in the last bin, for every participant and condition, as the definition of a PMA requires.

\paragraph{MBL}

MBL amplitude decreased before the response (Fig.~\ref{fig:pmaeeg}D). We do not interpret this change further, because the temporal resolution of MBL is too low for these short windows.

\paragraph{LRP}

The LRP reached its largest negative amplitude at the time of the response (slope: F(1,21) = 54.33, p < .001, $\eta_{p}^{2}$ = 0.721; Table~\ref{tab:lrp}). Neither coherence nor duration affected its level (all p > .5), and the duration effect on its slope did not reach significance (F(1,21) = 4.13, p = .055).


\section{Discussion}

%DISC-BEGIN
A central question for evidence accumulation models is how a decision is preserved when commitment and action are separated in time. A simple possibility is that the choice formed during stimulus presentation is stored and released when action is permitted. The present findings argue against this passive storage-and-release view. Replicating previous work \parencites{achardExtendingContinuousFlow2025}{roggeMotorCorticalSignals2022}, mean RT increased as motion coherence or duration decreased, even though RT was measured from RS onset, 3 s after stimulus offset. Delayed report latency therefore remained tied to prior motion evidence.

This study identifies the motor-preparatory mechanisms underlying that effect. EMG gave single-trial access to two events: PMAs, a time-resolved proxy of motor-preparatory activity, and the onset of response-related muscle activation, which fractionates RT into PMT and MT \parencites{colesPsychophysiologicalInvestigationContinuous}{servantIntegratedTheoryDeciding2021}. PMAs were nearly absent in the opposite channel but clearly expressed in the response-associated one, indicating effector-selective preparation rather than nonspecific muscular noise. The PMA rate increased during stimulus presentation with a buildup rate that scaled with motion coherence, consistent with evidence accumulation at the motor-preparatory level, as also described in non-human primate reaching decisions and in human motor cortex \parencites{cisekNeuralCorrelatesReaching2005}{donnerBuildupChoicePredictiveActivity2009}{kellyInternalExternalInfluences2013}{roggeMotorCorticalSignals2022}{selenDeliberationMotorSystem2012}; it remained elevated throughout the delay, indicating a sustained motor preparatory state; and it decreased after RS onset, consistent with a second accumulation phase that adds drive to this state and pushes activity toward an overt response. These observations support the core proposal that sensory evidence is accumulated and transmitted to motor circuits, where it is maintained until action is permitted. The distinction between a selective maintained state and the non-selective drive that culminates in the response has circuit-level analogues: in mice, subcortical control signals reshape motor-planning dynamics and promote transitions toward movement execution \parencite{inagakiNeuralAlgorithmsCircuits2022}, while in primates preparatory activity can occupy output-null dimensions that do not immediately drive motor output \parencite{churchlandPreparatoryActivityExpansive2024}.

RT fractionation then linked this preparatory state to delayed report latency, explaining why motion coherence and duration modulate mean RT. Within the theory, PMT indexes the starting point of the second accumulation phase relative to gating inhibition, whereas MT is the time from muscle-activation onset to response. That coherence and duration modulated mean PMT but not mean MT localizes the effects to processes preceding muscle-activation onset, and therefore to the starting point of the second accumulation phase. Both models predict this identically: temporal preparation is anchored to stimulus offset and the RS follows at a fixed interval, so it contributes the same amount in every condition (Results). Accuracy, RT, PMT and MT therefore constrain the two-stage architecture the models share, and do not choose between them.

The critical question is therefore why this starting point varied across conditions. Two windows of the PMA rate bear on it. During stimulus presentation, the rate was higher after the short than after the long stimulus (0.057 s$^{-1}$ [0.026, 0.088]). Model 1 has no temporal preparation; at 227 of its 228 admissible vectors it produces a higher rate after the long stimulus, never the observed one, and the same holds for the slope of the rate within that window. During the delay, the PMA rate ramped throughout, without the coherence-dependent buildup rate seen during the stimulus. Model 1 predicts no delay-window ramp at all, since its rate is constant across the window at every parameter value, whereas temporal preparation for the RS produces one, in line with work on anticipatory motor activity \parencites{burleExcitatoryInhibitoryMotor2010}{heidemanTemporalAlignmentAnticipatory2018}{ramayyaHumanResponseTimes2025}. Motion duration also affected accuracy, not just latency: accuracy was higher in the 2-s than the 0.3-s condition at intermediate coherence, so longer stimuli improved the evidence-dependent state itself, not merely the time available for temporal preparation.

The coherence $\times$ duration interaction on the delay-window PMA rate further specifies how duration shaped the state inherited from the first phase. A longer stimulus lets more trials reach the internal threshold and stop there \parencites{mazurekRoleNeuralIntegrators2003}{kianiBoundedIntegrationParietal2008}, which is why the coherence-related differences in the maintenance target shrink after a 2-s stimulus, as the account of PMT and RT above requires. The same logic predicts that the coherence effect on the delay-window PMA rate should also shrink, unless the 0\%-coherence, 2-s condition has itself mostly not reached the threshold. The data show the reverse: the coherence effect was larger after the 2-s stimulus. Most admissible vectors of both models produce the opposite sign (206 of 228 in Model 1, 188 of 204 in Model 2), but 10 vectors in each model reproduce the observed magnitude. This interaction therefore restricts the admissible parameters without separating the two models.

The EEG results complement this picture. MBL tracked motion-evidence accumulation early in the stimulus-onset window, with a coherence-dependent buildup rate confined to the first second before saturating; MBL then remained sustained during the delay while continuing to increase, consistent with alpha-/beta-band lateralization carrying decision-related information into motor cortex before response implementation \parencites{roggeMotorCorticalSignals2022}{twomeyAbstractEffectorSelectiveDecision2016}, and refined here: that sustained state is also shaped by temporal preparation for the expected RS. The LRP did not reliably track motion-evidence accumulation, but ramped during the delay, continued after RS onset, and peaked near the response: temporal preparation was not confined to upstream representations but also shaped later-stage effector-selective preparation.

Of the three measures, the PMA rate was the most sensitive readout of motor preparation here: neither EEG measure revealed the full coherence- and duration-dependent structure it did across the stimulus-onset, delay and RS windows, likely because PMAs are single-trial events measured directly from muscle. The PMA rate is therefore a promising tool for studying how decision evidence, temporal expectation and action constraints jointly shape motor preparation \parencites{balsdonSecondaryMotorIntegration2023}{dendauwGatedCascadeDiffusion2024}{ramayyaHumanResponseTimes2025}{roggeMotorCorticalSignals2022}{servantIntegratedTheoryDeciding2021}.

What the parameter-space evaluation establishes is a difference in what the two models can produce at all. With the maintenance fluctuation already at its stationary amplitude when maintenance begins, the model without temporal preparation has a PMA rate that is constant across the delay at every value of every parameter (Appendix, Corollary~\ref{cor:flat}), whereas the observed rate rises more than fourfold across the window. The same statement covers the shape, since a constant rate has zero curvature as well as zero slope, while the observed profile is both rising and convex. Leakage in the motion-evidence accumulator or in the RS-evidence accumulator changes the maintenance target but not the maintenance dynamics, so this prediction is unchanged, and no combination of the two leakage rates we examined changes whether a model reproduces an effect (Appendix, Remark~\ref{rem:leak}). The PMA rate was the only measure that separated the two models quantitatively; MBL and the LRP separate them only by the presence or absence of a delay-window ramp.

A scope condition concerns the coherence range: we sampled three levels (0\%, 12\%, and 50\%), coarser than the five- or six-level logarithmic series more commonly used to characterize psychometric and chronometric functions in random-dot motion studies \parencites[e.g., 3.2--51.2\%][]{roitmanResponseNeuronsLateral2002}[6.4--51.2\%][]{kianiBoundedIntegrationParietal2008}. This was sufficient to establish the coherence-dependent effects reported here, though not their full functional form. A second concerns delay length, fixed here at 3 s: companion data \parencite{achardExtendingContinuousFlow2025} show the same build-up preserved across 3--7 s delays when the response mapping is foreknown, but largely absent when revealed only at the RS.

In conclusion, delayed decision reports are not generated by storing an abstract completed choice and releasing it on demand. Instead, prior sensory evidence remains embodied in motor preparatory circuits, where it is maintained, reshaped by temporal expectations, and converted into overt action when the appropriate signal appears. By combining RT fractionation, PMA dynamics, and EEG markers of cortical motor preparation, the present study shows how evidence accumulation bridges perception, decision maintenance and action, reframing motor preparation as an active substrate rather than a passive buffer.

\section{Appendix. The rate of partial muscle activations (PMAs)}

This appendix derives the instantaneous rate at which the maintained motor-preparatory signal crosses the gate upward, which is a PMA, and the properties of that rate which the Results test.


\subsection{Setup and notation}

Notation follows the Theoretical framework (Materials and Methods) and Table~\ref{tab:params}; $\varphi$ and $\Phi$ are the standard normal density and distribution function. Fix a trial that has entered maintenance at time $\tau $ with selected response ${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}$ and maintenance target $m$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:m}), and write $\rho :=t-\tau $ for elapsed maintenance time. For $t\ge \tau $ let $y\left(t\right)=m+H\left(t\right)$, so that $H$ is the fluctuation of the motor-preparatory signal about the maintenance target. By Equations~\eqref{eq:epsilon} and~\eqref{eq:y_delay},

\begin{eqnarray}
    \dd \varepsilon \left(t\right) &=& -\kappa_{\varepsilon} \varepsilon \left(t\right)\, \dd t+{\sigma }_{\varepsilon }\, \dd {W}_{\varepsilon }\left(t\right), \qquad \varepsilon \left(\tau \right)\sim \mathrm{N}\left(0,{\mathrm{SD}}_{\varepsilon }^{2}\right), \nonumber \\
    \dd H\left(t\right) &= &\lambda \left(\varepsilon \left(t\right)-H\left(t\right)\right)\, \dd t
\label{eq:H}
\end{eqnarray}


with ${\sigma }_{\varepsilon }={\mathrm{SD}}_{\varepsilon }\sqrt{2\kappa_{\varepsilon} }$, so that ${\mathrm{SD}}_{\varepsilon }^{2}$ is the stationary variance of $\varepsilon$ and the maintenance noise begins in its own stationary state, independently of ${W}_{\varepsilon }$ and of the motion-evidence noise. By construction $H\left(\tau \right)=0$, since the filter output is continuous and $m$ is its value at $\tau $.

Since $\varepsilon$ is symmetric and independent of the sign of the response, ${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}H$ has the same law as $H$. Writing $\delta :={t}_{\mathrm{RS}}-{t}_{\mathrm{offset}}$ for the length of the preparation window and $d(t)$ for the distance from the maintenance target to the gate,

\begin{equation}
d\left(t\right)=g-\left|m\right|-\frac{\beta }{\delta }{\left(t-{t}_{\mathrm{offset}}\right)}^{+}
\label{eq:d}
\end{equation}


(the second term present only under temporal preparation, so that $\beta =0$ under Model 1), a PMA on the selected channel begins exactly when ${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}H$ crosses $d\left(t\right)$ upward. Temporal preparation therefore carries the signal toward the gate at the constant speed $\beta /\delta$. The non-selected channel activates when ${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}H$ falls below $-\left(g+\left|m\right|+\frac{\beta }{\delta }{\left(t-{t}_{\mathrm{offset}}\right)}^{+}\right)$. Since ${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}H$ has the same law as $-{s}_{\mathrm{resp}}H$, its rate is that of the selected channel with $d\left(t\right)$ replaced by this distance, which is larger than $d\left(t\right)$ by $2\left|m\right|+2\frac{\beta }{\delta }{\left(t-{t}_{\mathrm{offset}}\right)}^{+}$, and with the barrier moving away rather than toward the signal. In the stationary regime established below, its rate is therefore at most that of the selected channel, with equality only when $m=0$ and $\beta =0$; under Model 1 the ratio of the two rates is ${e}^{-2g\left|m\right|/{\sigma }_{H}^{2}}$. It is not considered further.

\subsection{The maintained fluctuation}

Solving Equation~\eqref{eq:H} gives

\begin{eqnarray*}
    \varepsilon \left(\tau +\rho \right)&=&\varepsilon \left(\tau \right){e}^{-\kappa_{\varepsilon} \rho }+{\sigma }_{\varepsilon }\int_{0}^{\rho } {e}^{-\kappa_{\varepsilon} \left(\rho -p\right)}\,\dd {W}_{\varepsilon }\left(p\right),\\ H\left(\tau +\rho \right)&=&\lambda \int_{0}^{\rho } {e}^{-\lambda \left(\rho -q\right)}\varepsilon \left(\tau +q\right)\, \dd q
\end{eqnarray*}


Since $\varepsilon$ admits a continuous modification and $H$ is then a Riemann integral of a continuous function, almost surely $H\in {C}^{1}$. Substituting the first line into the second and exchanging the order of integration (permissible because the integrand is bounded on $\{0\le p\le q\le \rho \}$) gives

\begin{eqnarray*}
    H\left(\tau +\rho \right)&=&\frac{\varepsilon \left(\tau \right)}{{\sigma }_{\varepsilon }}K\left(\rho \right)+\int_{0}^{\rho } K\left(\rho -p\right)\,
    \dd {W}_{\varepsilon }\left(p\right) \\
    K\left(v\right)&=&
    \begin{cases}
        \frac{\lambda {\sigma }_{\varepsilon }}{\lambda -\kappa_{\varepsilon} }\left({e}^{-\kappa_{\varepsilon} v}-{e}^{-\lambda v}\right), & \kappa_{\varepsilon} \neq \lambda, \\
\lambda {\sigma }_{\varepsilon }\,v\,{e}^{-\lambda v}, & \kappa_{\varepsilon} =\lambda.
        \end{cases}
\end{eqnarray*}


$H$ is therefore a centred Gaussian process, being a linear combination of the Gaussian $\varepsilon \left(\tau \right)$ and an independent Wiener integral. Note that $K\left(0\right)=0$, which makes the first term vanish at $\rho =0$ and recovers $H\left(\tau \right)=0$, and that ${K}^{'}\left(0\right)=\lambda {\sigma }_{\varepsilon }$. Differentiating under the integral and using $K\left(0\right)=0$ to kill the boundary term, $\dot{H}\left(\tau +\rho \right)=\left(\varepsilon \left(\tau \right)/{\sigma }_{\varepsilon }\right){K}^{'}\left(\rho \right)+\int_{0}^{\rho } {K}^{'}\left(\rho -p\right)\, \dd {W}_{\varepsilon }\left(p\right)$. Write ${\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(\rho \right):=\Var H\left(\tau +\rho \right)$, ${\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}\left(\rho \right):=\Var \dot{H}\left(\tau +\rho \right)$ and ${c}_{H}\left(\rho \right):=\Cov \left(H\left(\tau +\rho \right),\dot{H}\left(\tau +\rho \right)\right)$, and the same symbols without an argument for their limits as $\rho \to \infty $. Because $\varepsilon \left(\tau \right)$ and ${W}_{\varepsilon }$ are independent, the It\^o isometry gives each moment as the sum of the two contributions, ${\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(\rho \right)={K}^{2}\left(\rho \right)/2\kappa_{\varepsilon} +\int_{0}^{\rho } {K}^{2}$, ${\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}\left(\rho \right)={K}^{'2}\left(\rho \right)/2\kappa_{\varepsilon} +\int_{0}^{\rho } {K}^{'2}$ and ${c}_{H}\left(\rho \right)=K\left(\rho \right){K}^{'}\left(\rho \right)/2\kappa_{\varepsilon} +{K}^{2}\left(\rho \right)/2$. Evaluating them, and writing  ${E}_{1}\left(\rho \right):={e}^{-\left(\lambda +\kappa_{\varepsilon} \right)\rho }$, ${E}_{2}\left(\rho \right):={e}^{-2\lambda \rho }$, with  $X\left(\rho \right):=\frac{\lambda +\kappa_{\varepsilon} }{\lambda -\kappa_{\varepsilon} }\left({E}_{2}\left(\rho \right)-{E}_{1}\left(\rho \right)\right)$ if $\kappa_{\varepsilon} \neq \lambda$ and $X\left(\rho \right)=-2\lambda \rho {E}_{2}\left(\rho \right)$ if $\kappa_{\varepsilon} = \lambda$, the three moments are

  \begin{equation}\label{eq:moments}
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_H^2(\rho) &= \sigma_H^2\bigl(1+X(\rho)-E_1(\rho)\bigr)\\
c_H(\rho) &= -\lambda\,\sigma_H^2\,X(\rho)\\
\sigma_{\dot H}^2(\rho)
  &= \sigma_{\dot H}^2\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{\kappa_{\varepsilon}}\bigl(X(\rho)+E_1(\rho)\bigr)\right)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}


where the stationary limits, reached as $\rho \to \infty $ since ${E}_{1}\left(\rho \right),{E}_{2}\left(\rho \right)\to 0$, are

\begin{equation}\label{eq:asymp_moments}
{\sigma }_{H}^{2} = \frac{\lambda \, {\mathrm{SD}}_{\varepsilon }^{2}}{\lambda +\kappa_{\varepsilon} }, \quad {\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}= \frac{{\lambda }^{2}\kappa_{\varepsilon} \, {\mathrm{SD}}_{\varepsilon }^{2}}{\lambda +\kappa_{\varepsilon} }, \quad {c}_{H} = 0.
\end{equation}


Setting $\rho =0$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:moments} gives ${\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(0\right)=0$ and ${\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}\left(0\right)={\lambda }^{2}{\mathrm{SD}}_{\varepsilon }^{2}$: the fluctuation of the signal about the maintenance target starts at zero and fills in, while its derivative starts above its stationary value and falls. Temporal preparation is added outside the filter (Equation~\eqref{eq:tildey}), so it shifts the mean of the signal reaching the gate and leaves all three moments untouched; Equations~\eqref{eq:moments} and~\eqref{eq:asymp_moments} hold for both models.

\begin{proposition}[the analysis window lies in the stationary regime]\label{prop:stationary}
Let $\rho \ge 0.8$ s. Then, over the ranges of Table~\ref{tab:params}, ${E}_{1}\left(\rho \right)<3\times {10}^{-4}$ and $\left|X\left(\rho \right)\right|<2.4\times {10}^{-3}$, whatever $\kappa_{\varepsilon}$. The transient terms of Equation~\eqref{eq:moments} are therefore negligible on the delay analysis window, and the three moments may be replaced there by their stationary values, Equation~\eqref{eq:asymp_moments}.
\end{proposition}


\begin{proof}
Time enters Equation~\eqref{eq:moments} through ${E}_{1}\left(\rho \right)$, ${E}_{2}\left(\rho \right)$ and $X\left(\rho \right)$, and through nothing else. The first two decay at the rates $\lambda +\kappa_{\varepsilon} $ and $2\lambda $, both at least

\begin{equation*}
\mu :=\lambda +\min \left(\lambda ,\kappa_{\varepsilon} \right)\ge \lambda ,
\end{equation*}


and so does the third: applying the mean value theorem to $z\mapsto {e}^{-z\rho }$ between $z=\lambda +\kappa_{\varepsilon} $ and $z=2\lambda $ gives $\left|{E}_{2}\left(\rho \right)-{E}_{1}\left(\rho \right)\right|\le \left|\lambda -\kappa_{\varepsilon} \right|\rho {e}^{-\mu \rho }$, whence $\left|X\left(\rho \right)\right|\le \left(\lambda +\kappa_{\varepsilon} \right)\rho {e}^{-\mu \rho }$. Over the sampled ranges both bounds are largest at $\rho =0.8$ s, $\lambda =10$ s\textsuperscript{-1} and $\kappa_{\varepsilon} =0.2$ s\textsuperscript{-1}, where ${E}_{1}\left(\rho \right)\le {e}^{-8.16}<3\times {10}^{-4}$ and $\left|X\left(\rho \right)\right|\le 8.16\, {e}^{-8.16}<2.4\times {10}^{-3}$.
\end{proof}


Every mode of the transient therefore decays at least as fast as the filter gain $\lambda$, whatever $\kappa_{\varepsilon}$ is: $\kappa_{\varepsilon}$ appears in $\mu$, but only in the direction that makes the decay faster, since $\mu$ increases with $\kappa_{\varepsilon}$ up to $2\lambda $. Maintenance begins no later than ${t}_{\mathrm{offset}}={L}_{\mathrm{motion}}+D$ and the delay analysis window opens 1 s after the physical stimulus offset $D$, so $\rho \ge 1-{L}_{\mathrm{motion}}=0.8$ s throughout that window at every parameter setting sampled, and every statement below is made at the stationary moments.

\subsection{The crossing rate}

Throughout this subsection the distance to the gate is a function of $t$ and the moments of Equation~\eqref{eq:moments} are functions of the elapsed maintenance time $\rho =t-\tau $. Let $Z\left(t\right):={s}_{\mathrm{resp}}H\left(t\right)-d\left(t\right)$, so that upcrossings of $d$ by ${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}H$ are upcrossings of zero by $Z$, and $\dot{Z}=\dot{H}+\beta /\delta$. Trajectories that reach the response criterion before the end of the delay are neglected; they are premature responses, excluded from the empirical analysis and held to a small fraction of trials by the admissibility screen. Since $H$ is almost surely continuously differentiable and $\left(Z,\dot{Z}\right)$ is jointly Gaussian and non-degenerate for $\rho >0$, the Kac--Rice formula gives the expected upcrossing rate \parencite[ch. 3]{azaisLevelSetsExtrema2009}:

\begin{equation}
\nu \left(t\right)={p}_{Z\left(t\right)}\left(0\right)\, \mathrm{E}\left[\dot{Z}\left(t\right){1}_{\{\dot{Z}\left(t\right)>0\}} \mid Z\left(t\right)=0\right]
\label{eq:nu}
\end{equation}


Since $H$ is centred, $Z\left(t\right)\sim \mathrm{N}\left(-d\left(t\right),{\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(\rho \right)\right)$ and ${p}_{Z\left(t\right)}\left(0\right)=\varphi \left(d\left(t\right)/{\sigma }_{H}\left(\rho \right)\right)/{\sigma }_{H}\left(\rho \right)$. Because $\left(Z,\dot{Z}\right)$ is jointly Gaussian, $\dot{Z}$ given $Z=0$ is Gaussian with mean and variance

\begin{equation*}
M\left(t\right)=\frac{\beta }{\delta }+\frac{{c}_{H}\left(\rho \right)}{{\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(\rho \right)}d\left(t\right), \quad S\left(t\right)={\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}\left(\rho \right)-\frac{{c}_{H}{\left(\rho \right)}^{2}}{{\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(\rho \right)}
\end{equation*}


and for $\xi \sim \mathrm{N}\left(M,S\right)$ the expected positive part is $\mathrm{E}\left[\xi {1}_{\{\xi >0\}}\right]=M\Phi \left(M/\sqrt{S}\right)+\sqrt{S}\varphi \left(M/\sqrt{S}\right)$. Substituting into Equation~\eqref{eq:nu} gives the rate in general form,

\begin{equation}
\nu \left(t\right)=\frac{\varphi \left(d\left(t\right)/{\sigma }_{H}\left(\rho \right)\right)}{{\sigma }_{H}\left(\rho \right)}\left[M\left(t\right)\Phi \left(\frac{M\left(t\right)}{\sqrt{S\left(t\right)}}\right)+\sqrt{S\left(t\right)}\varphi \left(\frac{M\left(t\right)}{\sqrt{S\left(t\right)}}\right)\right]
\label{eq:nu2}
\end{equation}


Replacing the moments by their stationary values, which Proposition~\ref{prop:stationary} licenses on the analysis window, sets ${c}_{H}=0$ and therefore $M=\beta /\delta $ and $S={\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}$: both are constants, and all the time dependence is carried by the distance to the gate. Writing

\begin{equation*}
a\left(t\right):=\frac{d\left(t\right)}{{\sigma }_{H}}, \quad \omega :=\frac{{\sigma }_{\dot{H}}}{{\sigma }_{H}}=\sqrt{\lambda \kappa_{\varepsilon} }
\end{equation*}


for the distance to the gate in units of the fluctuation and for the characteristic frequency of the fluctuation, Equation~\eqref{eq:nu2} becomes the \emph{stationary rate}

\begin{equation}
\nustat \left(t\right)=\varphi \left(a\left(t\right)\right)\left[\frac{\beta }{\delta {\sigma }_{H}}\Phi \left(\frac{\beta }{\delta {\sigma }_{\dot{H}}}\right)+\omega \varphi \left(\frac{\beta }{\delta {\sigma }_{\dot{H}}}\right)\right]
\label{eq:nu_asymp}
\end{equation}


The bracket does not depend on the distance to the gate, and temporal preparation enters it only through $\beta /\delta$, the speed at which temporal preparation carries the signal toward the gate. The stationary rate is thus a product of two factors: a Gaussian in the standardized distance to the gate, which is where the conditions and the time within the delay act, and a bracket that depends on neither the motion coherence nor its duration.

\begin{corollary}[error of the stationary rate]\label{cor:approx}
Let $t$ be a time in the delay analysis window and $\rho =t-\tau $ the elapsed maintenance time. Write the three moments of Equation~\eqref{eq:moments} in terms of their departures from their stationary values,
\begin{equation*}
{\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(\rho \right)={\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(1+u\left(\rho \right)\right), \quad {\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}\left(\rho \right)={\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}\left(1+v\left(\rho \right)\right), \quad {c}_{H}\left(\rho \right)=w\left(\rho \right){\sigma }_{H}{\sigma }_{\dot{H}},
\end{equation*}
so that $u$ and $v$ are relative errors and $w$ is the whole of the error on ${c}_{H}$, whose stationary value is zero. By Equation~\eqref{eq:moments},
\begin{equation*}
u\left(\rho \right)=X\left(\rho \right)-{E}_{1}\left(\rho \right), \quad v\left(\rho \right)=\frac{\lambda }{\kappa_{\varepsilon} }\left(X\left(\rho \right)+{E}_{1}\left(\rho \right)\right), \quad w\left(\rho \right)=-X\left(\rho \right)\sqrt{\lambda /\kappa_{\varepsilon} }.
\end{equation*}
Set $\eta \left(\rho \right):=\max \left(\left|u\left(\rho \right)\right|,\left|v\left(\rho \right)\right|,\left|w\left(\rho \right)\right|\right)$. If ${\left(a\left(t\right)+2\right)}^{2}\eta \left(\rho \right)\le 1/5$ then
\begin{equation*}
\left|\ln \frac{\nu \left(t\right)}{\nustat \left(t\right)}\right|\le {\left(a\left(t\right)+2\right)}^{2}\eta \left(\rho \right).
\end{equation*}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Abbreviate $a=a\left(t\right)$, $\eta =\eta \left(\rho \right)$, and likewise for $u$, $v$ and $w$. Write $g\left(m\right):=m\Phi \left(m\right)+\varphi \left(m\right)$, so that Equation~\eqref{eq:nu2} reads $\nu \left(t\right)=\varphi \left({a}_{\rho }\right)B/{\sigma }_{H}\left(\rho \right)$ with ${a}_{\rho }:=d\left(t\right)/{\sigma }_{H}\left(\rho \right)$ and $B:=\sqrt{S}\, g\left(M/\sqrt{S}\right)$, and $\nustat \left(t\right)$ is the same expression at $u=v=w=0$. Note $X\left(\rho \right)\le 0$, so $w\ge 0$ and $M\ge \beta /\delta \ge 0$, whence $g\ge g\left(0\right)=\varphi \left(0\right)$; and ${g}^{'}=\Phi \in \left(0,1\right]$. The hypothesis gives $\eta \le 1/20$, so $1/\left(1-\eta \right)\le 5/4$ throughout.

Take the three factors in turn. Since ${a}_{\rho }^{2}={a}^{2}/\left(1+u\right)$, $\left|\ln \varphi \left({a}_{\rho }\right)/\varphi \left(a\right)\right|=\left({a}^{2}/2\right)\left|u\right|/\left(1+u\right)\le \tfrac{5}{8}{a}^{2}\eta$, and $\left|\ln {\sigma }_{H}/{\sigma }_{H}\left(\rho \right)\right|=\tfrac{1}{2}\left|\ln \left(1+u\right)\right|\le \tfrac{5}{8}\eta$.

For the bracket, $M=\beta /\delta +w{\sigma }_{\dot{H}}a/\left(1+u\right)$ and $S={\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}\left(1+v-{w}^{2}/\left(1+u\right)\right)$, so $\left|\Delta M\right|/{\sigma }_{\dot{H}}\le \tfrac{5}{4}a\eta$ and $\left|\Delta \sqrt{S}\right|/{\sigma }_{\dot{H}}\le \left|S/{\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}-1\right|\le \tfrac{5}{4}\eta$. Now $\partial B/\partial M=\Phi \left(M/\sqrt{S}\right)\le 1$ and $\partial B/\partial \sqrt{S}=\varphi \left(M/\sqrt{S}\right)\le \varphi \left(0\right)$, so by the mean value theorem $\left|\Delta B\right|\le \left|\Delta M\right|+\varphi \left(0\right)\left|\Delta \sqrt{S}\right|$; and ${B}_{\mathrm{stat}}\ge {\sigma }_{\dot{H}}\varphi \left(0\right)$. Hence $\left|\Delta B\right|/{B}_{\mathrm{stat}}\le \tfrac{5}{4}\sqrt{2\pi }a\eta +\tfrac{5}{4}\eta \le 1/4$ and $\left|\ln B/{B}_{\mathrm{stat}}\right|\le \tfrac{4}{3}\left|\Delta B\right|/{B}_{\mathrm{stat}}\le \tfrac{5}{3}\sqrt{2\pi }a\eta +\tfrac{5}{3}\eta$.

Adding the three, $\left|\ln \nu /\nustat \right|\le \left(\tfrac{5}{8}{a}^{2}+\tfrac{5}{3}\sqrt{2\pi }a+\tfrac{55}{24}\right)\eta \le \left({a}^{2}+4a+4\right)\eta$.
\end{proof}

The moment error is therefore amplified by the squared standardized distance to the gate: $a\left(t\right)$ enters the rate through a Gaussian, so what the transient does to ${\sigma }_{H}$ it does to the rate ${a\left(t\right)}^{2}/2$ times over. Over the admissible parameter vectors, $\eta \left(\rho \right)$ stays below $2\times {10}^{-3}$ everywhere in the window. Evaluated exactly, the largest relative departure of $\nu \left(t\right)$, Equation~\eqref{eq:nu2} at the moments of Equation~\eqref{eq:moments}, from $\nustat \left(t\right)$, Equation~\eqref{eq:nu_asymp}, over the admissible parameter vectors and the whole window, is $3\times {10}^{-3}$ under Model 1 and $1.2\times {10}^{-3}$ under Model 2 (Figure~\ref{fig:approx}). Over the whole sampled box it reaches 20\%, on parameter vectors whose standardized distance to the gate is close to 40 and whose rate is therefore far too small to be measured at all; admissibility caps that distance, and with it the amplification.

\begin{corollary}[under Model 1 the delay-window rate is constant]\label{cor:flat}
Under Model 1, at every parameter setting and at every time in the delay analysis window,

\begin{equation}
\nustat =\frac{\omega }{2\pi }{e}^{-{a}^{2}/2}, \quad a=\frac{g-\left|m\right|}{{\sigma }_{H}}
\label{eq:nu1}
\end{equation}


which does not depend on $t$; in particular $a\left(t\right)$ is the constant $a$. Every linear or quadratic contrast of the stationary rate across the window is therefore zero, for every $\kappa_{\varepsilon}$, every $\lambda$ and every maintenance target.

\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$\beta =0$ makes $d\left(t\right)$ constant by Equation~\eqref{eq:d}, so $a\left(t\right)$ is constant. Putting $M=0$ and $S={\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:nu_asymp} leaves $\nustat =\varphi \left(a\right){\sigma }_{\dot{H}}\varphi \left(0\right)/{\sigma }_{H}=\omega {e}^{-{a}^{2}/2}/2\pi $, the classical Rice formula.
\end{proof}


\begin{corollary}[under Model 2 the rate ramps, and the log rate is a parabola]\label{cor:logslope}
Under Model 2, on the analysis window,

\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd \left(\ln \nustat \right)}{\dd t}\left(t\right)=\frac{\beta }{\delta {\sigma }_{H}}a\left(t\right), \quad \frac{{\dd }^{2}\left(\ln \nustat \right)}{\dd {t}^{2}}\left(t\right)=-{\left(\frac{\beta }{\delta {\sigma }_{H}}\right)}^{2}
\label{eq:slope}
\end{equation}


so that the rate itself rises, $\dd \nustat /\dd t=\nustat \left(t\right)\left(\beta /\delta {\sigma }_{H}\right)a\left(t\right)>0$, wherever the signal has not yet reached the gate. Let $N\left(t\right)$ be the mean of $\nustat \left(t\right)$ over the trials of a condition, write $b:=\beta /\left(\delta {\sigma }_{H}\right)$, and let ${\E}_{\nu }$ and ${\Var}_{\nu }$ be the mean and the variance of $a\left(t\right)$ over those trials, each trial weighted by its own rate $\nustat \left(t\right)$. Then
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\frac{\dd \left(\ln N\right)}{\dd t}\left(t\right)=b\,{\E}_{\nu }\left[a\left(t\right)\right], \qquad \frac{{\dd }^{2}\left(\ln N\right)}{\dd {t}^{2}}\left(t\right)=-{b}^{2}\left(1-{\Var}_{\nu }\left[a\left(t\right)\right]\right),\\ \frac{{\dd }^{2}N}{\dd {t}^{2}}\left(t\right)={b}^{2}N\left(t\right)\left({\E}_{\nu }\left[{a\left(t\right)}^{2}\right]-1\right).
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:slope_cond}
\end{equation}

\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The barrier moves at constant speed, so by Equation~\eqref{eq:d} the standardized distance falls linearly, $a\left(t\right)=a\left({t}_{\mathrm{offset}}\right)-\beta \left(t-{t}_{\mathrm{offset}}\right)/\left(\delta {\sigma }_{H}\right)$. In Equation~\eqref{eq:nu_asymp} the bracket does not depend on $t$ and $\ln \varphi \left(a\right)=-{a}^{2}/2-\ln \sqrt{2\pi }$, so $\dd \left(\ln \nustat \right)/\dd t=-a\dot{a}$ with $\dot{a}=-\beta /\left(\delta {\sigma }_{H}\right)$. Differentiating once more gives the curvature.

For the mean rate, the trials of a condition differ in their maintenance target, hence in $a\left(t\right)$, but share $\beta $, $\delta $, ${\sigma }_{H}$ and the bracket of Equation~\eqref{eq:nu_asymp}, which are properties of the participant; so every trial satisfies $\dd \nustat /\dd t=b\,a\,\nustat $ with $\dot{a}=-b$. Averaging over trials gives $\dd N/\dd t=b\,\E\left[a\,\nustat \right]=b\,N\,{\E}_{\nu }\left[a\right]$, by the definition of the rate-weighted mean, which is the first identity after division by $N$. Differentiating each trial's identity once more gives ${\dd }^{2}\nustat /\dd {t}^{2}=b\left(\dot{a}\,\nustat +a\,\dd \nustat /\dd t\right)={b}^{2}\nustat \left({a}^{2}-1\right)$, and averaging gives the third identity. The second follows from ${\dd }^{2}\left(\ln N\right)/\dd {t}^{2}=\left({\dd }^{2}N/\dd {t}^{2}\right)/N-{\left(\left(\dd N/\dd t\right)/N\right)}^{2}={b}^{2}\left({\E}_{\nu }\left[{a}^{2}\right]-1-{\E}_{\nu }{\left[a\right]}^{2}\right)$.
\end{proof}

Three consequences are used in the Results. The rise of the rate is proportional to the rate: the slope of the mean rate is the product of its level and of its relative slope, so reproducing the observed value of that slope requires a model to produce both, and neither one alone. The log-rate slope of a condition is proportional to the rate-weighted mean standardized distance to the gate, so a condition whose crossings come from trials closer to the gate has a shallower log-rate slope. And the mean rate is convex wherever ${\E}_{\nu }\left[{a}^{2}\right]>1$, in particular wherever every contributing trial stands more than one fluctuation standard deviation from the gate. On a single trial the log rate is a downward parabola of curvature $-{b}^{2}$; over the trials of a condition its curvature is $-{b}^{2}\left(1-{\Var}_{\nu }\left[a\right]\right)$, which depends on the condition through the spread of the maintenance targets.


\begin{proposition}[the stimulus-onset-window rate under the two durations]\label{prop:duration}
Under either model, a trial that has not entered maintenance produces no PMA. Let ${D}_{s}:=0.3$ s, ${t}_{s}:={L}_{\mathrm{motion}}+{D}_{s}$, let $\tau $ be the first time at which $\left|y\right|$ reaches $\theta $ under the accumulation of the long stimulus, and let ${\nu }_{\ell }\left(\rho \right)$ be the rate of Equation~\eqref{eq:nu2} for a trial maintained at level $\ell $ for a time $\rho $. Under Model 1, the stimulus-onset-window rates after the long and the short stimulus, ${N}_{L}\left(t\right)$ and ${N}_{S}\left(t\right)$, are equal for $t\le {t}_{s}$, and for ${t}_{s}<t\le 2$ s
\begin{equation}
{N}_{L}\left(t\right)-{N}_{S}\left(t\right)=\E\left[{\nu }_{\theta }\left(t-\tau \right){\1}_{\left\{{t}_{s}<\tau \le t\right\}}\right]-\E\left[{\nu }_{\left|y\left({t}_{s}\right)\right|}\left(t-{t}_{s}\right){\1}_{\left\{\tau >{t}_{s}\right\}}\right].
\label{eq:duration}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Maintenance begins at the first time $\left|y\right|$ reaches $\theta $ (Equation~\eqref{eq:m}). Before it, $\left|y\right|<\theta <g$ by continuity of $y$, and temporal preparation has not begun, since it starts at ${t}_{\mathrm{offset}}\ge \tau $; so the drive $\left|\tilde{y}\right|-g$ is negative and no activation occurs. This proves the first statement.

Up to ${t}_{s}$ the two conditions follow the same equations, so their trajectories can be taken equal on $\left[0,{t}_{s}\right]$, and the two rates coincide there. Fix $t\in \left({t}_{s},2\right]$; the long stimulus is still being accumulated at $t$, since its offset is at ${L}_{\mathrm{motion}}+2$ s. Split the trials by $\tau $. If $\tau \le {t}_{s}$, the trial is maintained at $\theta $ from $\tau $ in both conditions and contributes ${\nu }_{\theta }\left(t-\tau \right)$ to both rates. If ${t}_{s}<\tau \le t$, it is maintained at $\theta $ from $\tau $ after the long stimulus and contributes ${\nu }_{\theta }\left(t-\tau \right)$; after the short stimulus it has been maintained at $\left|y\left({t}_{s}\right)\right|<\theta $ since ${t}_{s}$ and contributes ${\nu }_{\left|y\left({t}_{s}\right)\right|}\left(t-{t}_{s}\right)$. If $\tau >t$, it is still accumulating after the long stimulus and contributes nothing, by the first statement, while after the short stimulus it contributes ${\nu }_{\left|y\left({t}_{s}\right)\right|}\left(t-{t}_{s}\right)$. In each case the contribution is the Kac--Rice rate of the maintained fluctuation, which is independent of the accumulation that set the level and the onset. Taking expectations and subtracting gives Equation~\eqref{eq:duration}.
\end{proof}

Both terms of Equation~\eqref{eq:duration} are non-negative. The first is the rate of the trials that reach $\theta $, the level closest to the gate, during the further accumulation of the long stimulus; it is present at every coherence, including $c=0$, where trials reach $\theta $ by diffusion. The second is the rate of the trials that the short stimulus left maintained below $\theta $. Their difference has no sign fixed by the model: the second term dominates when the gate is close to $\theta $ in units of ${\sigma }_{H}$, so that trials maintained below $\theta $ cross it often. Over Model 1's admissible parameter vectors the first term dominates: the simulated stimulus-onset-window rate is significantly higher after the long stimulus at 227 of 228 vectors, and significantly higher after the short one at none. Under Model 2, temporal preparation adds to the short condition from ${t}_{\mathrm{offset}}$ onward, inside the window, and pushes the difference the other way.

\subsection{Additional remarks}


\begin{remark}[leaks in the two accumulators]\label{rem:leak}
Suppose the motion-evidence accumulation of Equation~\eqref{eq:x} and the response-signal accumulation of Equation~\eqref{eq:q} each carry a leak, $\dd x\left(t\right)=\left(skc-{\gamma }_{x}x\left(t\right)\right)\dd t+{\sigma }_{x}\dd {W}_{x}\left(t\right)$ and $\dd q\left(t\right)=\left({\mu }_{\mathrm{RS}}-{\gamma }_{q}q\left(t\right)\right)\dd t+{\sigma }_{q}\dd {W}_{q}\left(t\right)$, with ${\gamma }_{x},{\gamma }_{q}\ge 0$. Every delay-window statement above holds at every ${\gamma }_{x}$ and ${\gamma }_{q}$, with $a$ taking whatever value the leaked accumulation leaves. After the response signal, the conditions act on response time only through the value of the signal reaching the gate at ${t}_{\mathrm{RS}}$, at every ${\gamma }_{q}$.
\end{remark}

The leak ${\gamma }_{x}$ acts only before maintenance begins. It changes the law of $x$, hence the law of the threshold-crossing time $\tau $ and of the maintenance target $m$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:m}, and therefore the distribution across trials of the standardized distance $a$. It does not enter the maintenance dynamics: from $\tau $ onward the input to the filter is $m+\varepsilon \left(t\right)$ by Equation~\eqref{eq:y_delay}, and neither term depends on how $x$ arrived. So $H$, its moments (Equation~\eqref{eq:moments}) and the crossing rate (Equation~\eqref{eq:nu2}) are exactly as derived. The leak ${\gamma }_{q}$ acts only from ${t}_{\mathrm{RS}}$, where $q$ starts, and the delay analysis window ends before ${t}_{\mathrm{RS}}$. In particular Corollary~\ref{cor:flat} holds at every ${\gamma }_{x}$ and ${\gamma }_{q}$: a leak can move the level of Model 1's delay-window rate, not give it a time dependence.

After the response signal, solving Equation~\eqref{eq:y_RS} by variation of constants gives ${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}\tilde{y}\left({t}_{\mathrm{RS}}+u\right)={s}_{\mathrm{resp}}\tilde{y}\left({t}_{\mathrm{RS}}\right)+Q\left(u\right)$ for $u\ge 0$, where $Q\left(u\right):=\int_{0}^{u}\lambda {e}^{-\lambda \left(u-v\right)}q\left({t}_{\mathrm{RS}}+v\right)\dd v$; the term ${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}\beta $ of Model 2 is constant from ${t}_{\mathrm{RS}}$ onward and is part of ${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}\tilde{y}\left({t}_{\mathrm{RS}}\right)$. The process $Q$ starts from $q\left({t}_{\mathrm{RS}}\right)=0$, is driven by ${W}_{q}$ alone, and has parameters that do not depend on the condition, so it has the same law in every condition and is independent of everything before ${t}_{\mathrm{RS}}$. The response occurs when $Q$ first reaches $g+r-{s}_{\mathrm{resp}}\tilde{y}\left({t}_{\mathrm{RS}}\right)$, so on every path a larger ${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}\tilde{y}\left({t}_{\mathrm{RS}}\right)$ gives a response no later, whatever ${\gamma }_{q}$.

The decomposition of Proposition~\ref{prop:duration} uses no property of the accumulation beyond $\theta <g$, and holds at every ${\gamma }_{x}$; the sign of the difference it describes is numerical with or without a leak. Re-running the parameter vectors admissible in either model over ${\gamma }_{x},{\gamma }_{q}\in \left\{0,0.25,0.5,1\right\}$ s\textsuperscript{-1}, with the same number of trials, changes no verdict of the model--data comparison (Table~\ref{tab:comparison}).


\begin{remark}[the scope of the approximations]\label{rem:scope}
The quantity computed above is the expected upcrossing rate of an unabsorbed Gaussian process, and the quantity measured is neither of those things exactly. Three idealizations separate them.
\begin{enumerate}
\item In the simulations every upcrossing is counted, whereas in the recorded EMG, activity periods closer together than 25 ms are merged at detection. How often two upcrossings fall that close is set by the characteristic frequency $\omega =\sqrt{\lambda \kappa_{\varepsilon} }$. Under Model 1, merging would lower the rate by the same factor at every time in the window, so it cannot create a slope where the model predicts none.
\item The response criterion is an absorbing boundary: a delay-window crossing that reaches it ends the trial as a premature response, and conditioning on survival destroys the Gaussianity Equation~\eqref{eq:nu} rests on. Absorption removes preferentially the trials closest to the gate, and preferentially late in the window, so it depresses the late rate and lowers the measured slope below the computed one.
\item The activation that becomes the overt response is not a PMA and is excluded from the count wherever it begins. In the model it occasionally begins before the response signal, and therefore inside the last bins of the delay window; in the data this is essentially never so. This too removes events late in the window and lowers the measured slope.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}
The first idealization cannot create a slope under Model 1, and the last two lower the slope. What is derived above therefore bounds from above what either model can be measured to do on the delay-window slope, and the flatness of Corollary~\ref{cor:flat} is not an artefact of them.
