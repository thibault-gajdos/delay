\textbf{Abstract}

In delayed perceptual reports, the decision is formed before action is permitted, yet response time from the response signal still depends on sensory evidence. This paradox raises the possibility that evidence accumulation leaves a graded trace in motor preparatory circuits rather than ending in a discrete stored choice. We tested this hypothesis in 22 adults of both sexes (17 women) performing a response-signal random-dot motion task with electroencephalography and electromyography. Participants judged motion direction, withheld the selected response for 3 s, and executed it when the response signal was presented; motion coherence and duration were manipulated. Response time increased when coherence or duration decreased.  Fractionating response time at muscle-activation onset showed that these effects were confined to the premotor period. Muscle activations that did not produce overt responses provided a peripheral readout of motor preparation: they were effector-selective, built up during stimulus presentation at a coherence-dependent rate, and \new{continued to ramp during the stimulus-free delay, but no longer at a coherence-dependent build-up rate.} These results support an account in which accumulated motion evidence is transmitted to motor preparatory circuits, transformed into a sustained state maintaining the selected response, and shaped by temporal preparation before action is permitted. Electroencephalography revealed a hierarchy: mu--beta-band lateralization tracked motion-evidence accumulation and temporal preparation, whereas the lateralized readiness potential tracked temporal preparation and continued to build up toward the response. Thus, delayed decisions are preserved not as discrete stored choices, but as graded motor-preparatory states that remain dynamically regulated until action is permitted.

\textbf{Significance statement}

Perceptual decisions often cannot be reported immediately, yet the brain must preserve them until action is allowed. By combining brain and muscle recordings in humans, we show that, over short delays, information extracted from a stimulus is not simply converted into a fixed choice and released later. Instead, it remains graded in motor preparatory circuits, where it is maintained and adjusted by expectations about when action can occur. Muscle activations too weak to produce overt responses provided an especially sensitive window onto these hidden preparatory states, offering a novel tool for studying how decisions are maintained and regulated before behavior.


\section{Introduction}

Perceptual decision-making transforms sensory information into choices that guide action. Much of the human literature has used paradigms in which participants report a choice as soon as they commit to it \parencites{forstmannSequentialSamplingModels2016}{oconnellNeurophysiologyHumanPerceptual2021}, motivating neurally informed models in which noisy sensory evidence is accumulated and continuously conveyed to the motor system, allowing candidate actions to be prepared in parallel  \parencites{balsdonSecondaryMotorIntegration2023}{dendauwGatedCascadeDiffusion2024}{kellyNeurocomputationalMechanismsPriorinformed2021}.  But commitment and action are often separated in time, and how such models extend to decisions whose report must be withheld is unclear.

A canonical paradigm is the random-dot motion discrimination task, in which observers judge motion direction and report their choice. Recent variants impose a delay between stimulus offset and a response signal (RS), so the report must be withheld until prompted  \parencites{achardExtendingContinuousFlow2025}{MotorCorticalSignals}.  If evidence were converted into a discrete choice, stored, and merely released at the RS, mean response time (RT, from RS onset) should be insensitive to earlier stimulus characteristics. Yet mean RT is longer with lower motion discriminability and shorter motion duration, and these effects persist across delays of up to seven seconds \parencite{achardExtendingContinuousFlow2025}, making incomplete decision formation at RS onset unlikely.

\section{Materials and Methods}


\subsection{\new{Experimental Procedures}}


\subsubsection{Participants}

Twenty-two participants (17 women; mean age = 20.08; range 18--24) took part in the experiment in exchange for a \texteuro{}20 gift card.  The sample size was based on our previous study \parencite[Experiment 2]{achardExtendingContinuousFlow2025}, which provided  strong Bayesian evidence (inclusion Bayes factor > 10) that  stimulus discriminability and duration affect delayed-report latency.  Participants were students at  Universit\'e Marie et Louis Pasteur (France) recruited through online advertisements. All were right-handed, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and reported no history of motor, psychiatric, or neurological disorders. Participants were unaware of the study purpose and provided written informed consent. The university research ethics committee approved the study (Agreement CERUBFC-2022-01-18-002).


\subsubsection{Experimental task}

Participants performed a \new{response-signal (RS) variant of the} random-dot motion discrimination task. They judged  whether motion was leftward or rightward, maintained the selected response during a 3-s delay, and reported it \new{when the RS} appeared. Leftward and rightward judgments were reported with the corresponding thumb using two buttons mounted in a rubberized ergonomic grip (Black Box Toolkit).  Motion duration (0.3 or 2 s) and coherence (0\%, 12\%, or 50\%) varied factorially. The 0\% condition contained no net directional evidence and therefore indexed chance-level choices; 12\% and 50\% coherence provided intermediate and high discriminability, respectively.

The stimulus contained white dots (0.05$^{\circ}$ in diameter;  71 dots per frame on average) presented within a virtual circular aperture  (11.78$^{\circ}$ in diameter)  on a black background. On each frame, a proportion of dots moved coherently leftward or rightward at 10.5$^{\circ}$/s, whereas the remaining dots were repositioned randomly. Stimuli were displayed on a 34.42 $\times$ 19.36 cm LCD monitor (1920 $\times$ 1080; 60 Hz) viewed from 75 cm in a dimly lit room. Participants rested their hands on a cushion to minimize incidental muscle activity.

Participants completed 10 blocks of 96 trials, with self-paced breaks between blocks. Within each block, trials were defined by the factorial combination of motion direction, motion coherence, and motion duration (0.3 vs 2  s). Each trial type was presented eight times per block in randomized order, resulting in 160 trials per coherence $\times$ duration condition across the experiment.  The time course of each trial is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:design}A. A colored fixation cross remained at the center of the screen for the duration of the trial. After a variable prestimulus interval (0.6, 0.8, or 1  s, randomly selected on a trial-by-trial basis), the motion  stimulus appeared and was followed by a  3-s delay. A change in fixation color from blue to yellow served as the RS. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible after this change. If no response was made within 4 s of RS onset, the feedback message "Too late, please respond faster" was displayed for 2  s. If a response occurred before the RS, the feedback message "Too early! Please wait for the color change of the fixation cross" was displayed for 2  s. Feedback messages or valid responses were followed by a 1.5  s inter-trial interval, during which a schematic image of an eye was displayed. Participants were instructed to maintain fixation on the central cross and refrain from blinking  until the eye schematic appeared.  Trials with no response  (0.28\%;  participant range, 0--3.34\%), a response before the RS (1.37\%; range, 0.21--3.33\%), or a RT shorter than  0.2 s (0.32\%; range, 0--2.92\%) were excluded.


\subsubsection{EMG acquisition and preprocessing}

EMG activity from  the \emph{flexor pollicis brevis}, which flexes the thumb at the metacarpophalangeal joint and contributes to flexion at the carpometacarpal joint, was continuously recorded during task performance using two active Ag/AgCl electrodes placed 1 cm apart on the skin over the thenar eminence of each hand.  The electrodes were connected to a BioSemi Active  Two Mk2 biopotential measurement system (sampling rate = 1024 Hz).  After acquisition, the voltage difference between each electrode pair was computed. Left- and right-hand  bipolar EMG signals were then high-pass filtered at  10 Hz using a third-order Butterworth filter to remove slow drifts unrelated to muscle activity, full-wave rectified, and segmented from -0.3 to 9 s relative to stimulus onset.

Muscle activation onsets and offsets were detected using the three-step semi-automatic procedure described in \textcite{achardExtendingContinuousFlow2025}. \old{For each trial and EMG channel, the mean and} \old{standard deviation of the full-wave rectified signal were computed during the 300-ms} \old{prestimulus} \old{period. Candidate muscle-activity periods were identified when signal amplitude exceeded the mean by seven standard deviations.} \old{At this initial detection stage, threshold-defined periods separated by less than 25} \old{ms} \old{were merged into a single candidate burst to prevent brief within-burst drops below the conservative detection threshold from artificially splitting a single activation.}  \old{Candidate bursts were then filtered to limit false detections arising from background noise. Specifically, candidate bursts containing} \old{at most} \old{six above-threshold samples were counted} \old{across the epoch}\old{, and their occurrence rate per second was added to a base minimum of three} \old{above-threshold} \old{samples. Candidate bursts not meeting this adaptive criterion were discarded.}  \new{T}\old{he onset and offset of each} \old{retained} \old{candidate burst were then refined using the integrated-profile method}  \old{\parencites{liuUseIntegratedProfile2016}{santelloControlTimingAmplitude1998}}\new{.}  \old{For each burst, the integrated profile was computed from the cumulative rectified EMG in a window encompassing the burst and adjacent background activity, supplemented with additional} \old{background}  \old{EMG from the beginning and end of the epoch. The overall linear trend of this cumulative signal was then removed; inclusion of the additional background activity reduced the influence of the burst itself on the trend estimate. The resulting profile was smoothed using a 15-ms moving average, and its minimum and subsequent maximum were taken as estimates of burst} \old{onset and offset, respectively}\new{.}  \old{The search for the}\old{se extrema} \old{extended up to 50} \old{ms} \old{before the threshold-defined onset and 50} \old{ms} \old{aft}\old{er the threshold-defined offset}\new{.} \old{For neighboring candidate bursts, the search ranges were truncated at the midpoint of the interval between the threshold-defined offset of the first burst and the threshold-defined onset of the next, preventing overlap}\new{.} \old{The integrated-profile method improved the temporal localization of burst boundaries relative to the initial high-threshold detection, yielding more precis}\old{e estimates of onset and offset}\new{.}  \old{Finally, every trial was visually inspected and erroneous} \old{onset markers were manually corrected.}  Trials for which excessive noise prevented reliable onset detection were excluded from the analyses (0.18\%, range 0--1.04\%).

\new{Two further properties of this procedure matter for how partial muscle activations are counted and interpreted below. Because the integrated-profile refinement can only move a burst's boundaries earlier relative to the initial high-threshold detection, the final event list can contain activations separated by less than the 25-ms merge interval even though the merge rule was applied; this does not affect the reported counts, since the refinement relocates boundaries but does not change the number of intervals (27\% of consecutive activation pairs in the exported event list are separated by less than 25 ms). The response-period results (see Results) should be read with this possibility of segmentation in mind.}


\subsubsection{EMG-derived measures}

\old{We focused on three measures derived from the EMG signal: pre-motor execution time, motor execution time, and partial muscle activations. This choice builds on previous work in immediate-report decision tasks showing that the}\old{se measures provide strong constraints on models linking evidence accumulation to} \old{muscle activity}  \old{\parencites[e.g.][]{dendauwGatedCascadeDiffusion2024}[e.g.][]{dendauwSeparatingDecisionMotor2026}}\new{.}  \old{RT} \old{was fractionated according to the onset of the muscle activation leading to the button press (}\new{Fig.~\ref{fig:design}}\old{). Pre-motor execution time (PMET) was defined as the interval between RS onset and the onset of this response-related muscle activation, whereas motor execution time (MET) was the interval between muscle-activation onset and the button press.} \old{Partial muscle activations (PMAs) were defined as discrete muscle-activation episodes whose onset occurred after stimulus onset and before the button press but that did not constitute the fi}\old{nal response-related activation}\new{.} \old{Because partial muscle activations are discrete events, temporal binning provides a simple way to characterize how their occurrence evolves over the course of a trial.} \old{Each partial muscle activation was assigned to a 0.1-s bin according to its onset, and we computed the mean number of partial muscle activations per bin for each participant, condition, and EMG channel.} \new{T}\old{he 0.1-s bin width chosen as a compromise between temporal resolution and stability of the estimated event rate}\new{.} Channels were coded according to whether they corresponded to the selected or nonselected response.


\subsubsection{EEG acquisition and preprocessing}

EEG signals were recorded continuously during task performance from 64 active Ag/AgCl electrodes positioned according to the international 10--20 system, using the same BioSemi  ActiveTwo system and sampling rate as the EMG recordings.  Vertical and horizontal eye movements were recorded using electro-oculogram electrodes: two placed above and below the right eye to measure vertical movements, and two placed at the outer canthi of the eyes to measure horizontal movements. The data were preprocessed using custom scripts together with functions from the MNE-Python toolbox (v1.9.0). Horizontal and vertical electro-oculogram signals were derived by computing the voltage difference between the corresponding electrode pairs. EEG channels exhibiting  excessive variance or drifts were interpolated using the spherical spline method, and the data were re-referenced to the average of all electrodes.  Ocular artifacts were corrected using independent component analysis. For independent component analysis decomposition only, the continuous EEG data were high-pass filtered at 1 Hz to remove slow drifts and low-frequency noise that could impair artifact isolation, and then epoched from $-$0.6 to 9 s relative to stimulus onset. Independent component analysis was implemented using the default parameters of MNE's independent component analysis function. Components associated with eye movements were identified using MNE's  \emph{find\_bads\_eog}  function, which detects electro-oculogram-related components based on a z-score-transformed Pearson correlation between independent component analysis  components and electro-oculogram signals filtered in the 1--10 Hz range. Components with z-scores exceeding a threshold of 3 were masked at  each iteration, and z-scores were recalculated on the remaining components. This iterative procedure enables  adaptive identification of ocular artifacts and, together with visual inspection, ensured robust detection of eye-movement-related components. Up to four components were removed per participant.  The resulting independent component analysis  weights were then applied to  EEG data that had been filtered between 0.1 and 40 Hz before segmentation. All EEG and electro-oculogram  preprocessing filters were finite impulse response filters with a Hamming window. The epoched data were baseline-corrected using  the 0.1  s window preceding stimulus onset, and epochs were excluded from further analysis if any scalp channel exceeded an absolute amplitude of 150  $\mu$V  (\emph{M} = 10.1\%, range 1.35--21.98\%). Finally, the EEG data were transformed to current source density using the corresponding MNE function with default parameters, to reduce the spatial blurring effects of volume conduction \parencites[e.g.][]{kellyNeurocomputationalMechanismsPriorinformed2021}{kellyInternalExternalInfluences2013}{twomeyAbstractEffectorSelectiveDecision2016}. After applying EEG, EMG, and behavioral trial exclusion criteria, an average of 84.52\% of trials remained for analysis (range  48.44--98.33\%,  SD = 11.35).


\subsubsection{Measurement of motor preparation EEG signals}

EEG studies have identified two main markers of motor preparation: the lateralized readiness potential (LRP), a time-domain event-related potential, and mu--beta-band spectral amplitude lateralization (MBL), a time-frequency measure capturing asymmetric suppression of mu--beta oscillations over motor cortices. Both markers  are computed as difference waveforms by contrasting activity over motor areas contralateral and ipsilateral to the responding effector. The lateralized readiness potential is a negative-going potential that precedes unilateral movements and is commonly interpreted as reflecting effector-specific motor activation in contralateral motor-related regions,  indexing  late-stage response preparation \parencites[e.g.][]{eimerLateralizedReadinessPotential1998}{grattonPrePoststimulusActivation1988}. Mu--beta-band spectral amplitude lateralization  reflects the relative decrease in mu--beta amplitude (8--30 Hz) over the contralateral hemisphere as motor plans are formed, indexing the lateralized preparatory modulation of motor cortical activity  \parencite{pfurtschellerEventrelatedEEGMEG1999}. \old{We} \old{used these tw}\old{o markers}  \old{to provide additional} \old{constraints on th}\old{e motor}\new{-}\old{preparation stage of the} \old{model.}

To measure mu--beta-band spectral amplitude lateralization, we extracted  mu--beta amplitude  (8--30 Hz)  over time for each electrode by applying a short-time fast Fourier transform to the current source density signals from each epoch, using a 0.3-s window and a 0.1-s step.  To identify electrode sites exhibiting maximal effector-selective activity, we computed a grand-average topography for the -0.2 to 0 s interval relative to the response by subtracting, at each electrode site, the mean mu--beta amplitude for right-hand trials from that for left-hand trials, irrespective of experimental condition.  An analogous topography was  constructed using time-domain current source density  amplitudes. The resulting maps revealed overlapping extrema  at  electrodes C3 and C4 (Fig.~\ref{fig:pmaeeg}C,D), which were therefore  selected for subsequent analyses. Mu--beta-band spectral amplitude lateralization and the lateralized readiness potential were computed by subtracting, for each trial, the amplitude at the electrode ipsilateral to the responding hand from that at the contralateral electrode, mu--beta amplitude and current source density signals, respectively.

\old{The model specifies the neural drive transmitted to the response muscles, so the EMG-derived measures have explicit model counterparts; it does not specify a forward observation model relating its motor-preparatory variables to scalp potentials or spectral amplitude, so the EEG} \old{measures were used to constrain the temporal organization of motor preparation qualitatively rather than to classify parameterizations numerically.}


\subsubsection{Statistical Analysis}

Behavioral, EMG, and EEG data were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVAs, with the significance threshold set at $\alpha$ = .05. The sphericity assumption was evaluated using Mauchly's test, and violations were corrected with the Greenhouse-Geisser procedure.  To maximize statistical power, analyses included  both correct and incorrect trials; analyses restricted to correct trials yielded  comparable results. ANOVAs on response accuracy, mean RT, mean pre-motor execution time, and mean motor execution time  included motion coherence and motion duration as factors\new{; full results, with effect sizes, are in Table~\ref{tab:behav}}. \old{ANOVAs on partial motor activation rate included motion coherence, motion duration, and time bin as factors} \new{and were conducted separately across four periods of interest, allowing each period to be assessed against the corresponding model prediction (Results).} Each period was time-locked to one of the four main events within a trial: stimulus onset, stimulus offset, RS onset, and the response. The stimulus-onset-locked analysis covered the 2  s following stimulus onset (20 time bins). The stimulus-offset-locked analysis covered the interval from 1 to 3 s after stimulus offset (20 time bins), to avoid potentially redundant inclusion of motion-evidence accumulation in the 0.3 s duration condition. The RS-onset-locked analysis covered the 0.2 s following RS onset (2 time bins), ensuring equal trial counts across bins. Finally, the response-locked analysis focused on the 0.2 s preceding the button press (2 time bins).  In what follows, we refer to these four analysis windows as the \emph{stimulus}, \emph{delay}, \emph{RS}, and \emph{response}  \emph{periods}. \old{For consistency across neurophysiological measures,} \old{the lateralized readiness potential} and \old{mu--beta-band spectral amplitude lateralization} \old{were analyzed using the same 0.1-s temporal grid, with mean signal amplitude as the dependent variable in each bin.}  \old{For mu--beta-band spectral amplitude lateralization, spectral amplitude was estimated every 0.1 s using a 0.3-s sliding window, so the 0.1-s} \old{temporal grid preserved all available time points.}  \old{For the} \old{lateralized readiness potential}\old{, averaging within 0.1-s bins reduced the native temporal resolution of the signal but retained a resolution substantially finer than the task periods examined here.}

\new{For each participant and each of the six coherence $\times$ duration cells, the measure takes one value in each time bin of the period, and we summarized that series of values by fitting curves through it rather than by testing the bins against one another. Through the bin values we fitted a straight line by least squares and kept its \emph{slope}: how fast the measure rises or falls across the period, which is what we mean here by ramping. Where the period had at least three bins we also fitted a parabola and kept its \emph{curvature}: how far the profile bends away from that straight line, positive where it is U-shaped and negative where it rises and then levels off. These two numbers are the linear and quadratic orthogonal polynomial contrasts on the time-bin factor. Each was then treated as an ordinary dependent variable, one value per participant and cell, and tested twice: against zero, which asks whether the measure ramps (or bends) at all, and in its own duration $\times$ coherence repeated-measures ANOVA, which asks whether the ramp depends on the conditions. Greenhouse--Geisser correction was applied to the three-level coherence factor throughout. The curvature is reported alongside the slope because mu--beta lateralization during the stimulus period is non-monotone, rising over roughly the first second and then flattening, so a slope alone would misclassify it as null. Full results for every measure and period are in Tables~\ref{tab:pma}--\ref{tab:mbl}, whose duration, coherence and duration $\times$ coherence rows are the same tests applied to the mean over bins; analyses were conducted in R using the afex and emmeans packages. For delay-period partial-activation rate specifically, this analysis is further complemented by a fully unbinned, point-process analysis of exact activation onset times (Table~\ref{tab:pp}).}

\new{The response period's final 0.1-s bin ($-$0.1 to 0 s before the response) is, by construction, exactly zero for every participant and condition, because a partial activation is by definition an activation that precedes the response-producing burst, and that burst begins on average about 160 ms before the button press. Thus the response period is analyzed on its single informative bin.}

\subsubsection{\new{Evaluation over the parameter space}}

\new{The equations above were integrated numerically in Python (NumPy) by the explicit Euler--Maruyama scheme, at a fixed time step of 1/1024 s, the EMG and EEG acquisition rate, so that detection runs on the same time grid as the recorded signals. Each simulated trial yields one trace of the gated drive, from stimulus onset to the earlier of the response or the trial's end; the response time is the first time step at which the drive reaches the response criterion, after which the residual motor latency is added. Partial muscle activations are extracted from the trace by the rule applied to the recorded EMG (EMG acquisition and preprocessing, above): the drive crossing above zero, merging of intervals separated by 25 ms or less, and discarding merged intervals with fewer than an adaptively set minimum of supra-threshold samples. Trials are simulated independently per condition from a fixed pseudo-random seed.}

\new{The ranges sampled are given in Table~\ref{tab:params}. They come from the design of the experiment, from published estimates, from ranges observed in the data, or from the structure of the model. The model is invariant under a joint rescaling of every amplitude it contains, so one of them, $\sigma_{x}$, is held fixed to set the units and the remaining amplitudes are in effect expressed in units of it. The coherence scaling $k$ and the filter gain $\lambda$ were estimated by \textcite{dendauwGatedCascadeDiffusion2024} in an immediate-report task; because constants inherited from immediate report need not transfer to delayed report, both were sampled over roughly a factor of two either side of those estimates, as was the response criterion $r$. The residual motor latency $L_{\mathrm{motor}}$ is fixed at their 0.093 s, a peripheral electromechanical delay having better reason to transfer between tasks than a decision-related constant; the two encoding latencies $L_{\mathrm{motion}}$ and $L_{\mathrm{RS}}$ are fixed at 0.200 s. No prior study in this paradigm constrains $\kappa$, which was sampled over more than two orders of magnitude, from a maintenance fluctuation that stays correlated across the whole delay ($1/\kappa = 5$ s) to one that decorrelates within a single analysis bin (33 ms), a range generous in the direction that favors Model 1, whose only route to a rate still rising at the end of the delay is a small $\kappa$. The maintenance threshold is sampled as $\theta /g$, which keeps $\theta <g$ as the model requires, and the maintenance noise through its stationary standard deviation, so that fluctuation amplitude varies independently of $\kappa$.}


\new{Parameters were drawn by Latin-hypercube sampling over those ranges: 1200 draws, each evaluated in both models (the same vector in each, with $\beta$ set to zero in Model 1), and 4000 trials per cell per model per draw. A draw was retained as admissible if it satisfied the following requirements: in every condition, at most 10\% premature responses, at most 10\% responses within 0.2 s of the response signal, at most 5\% failures to respond by the 4-s deadline, and accuracy above 80\% in the two 50\%-coherence conditions; and, across conditions, a delay-period partial-activation rate between 0.04 and 3 s\textsuperscript{-1}, a mean premotor execution time between 181 and 677 ms and a mean motor execution time between 46 and 288 ms. The last three windows are the observed ranges of the per-participant means, widened by a factor of two about their centre; the activation-rate window is wider still than the observed 0.085 to 2.785 s\textsuperscript{-1}.}
\new{Figures~\ref{fig:dynamics} and~\ref{fig:design} are drawn at a single representative vector, the admissible draw closest to the centre of the admissible set.}

\new{Effect sizes are computed as follows. In the data, the six coherence $\times$ duration cell values are computed for each of the 22 participants from that participant's own trials and combined into the effect (a difference of means for a main effect, a difference of such differences for an interaction); the 22 resulting values are averaged, their standard deviation divided by $\sqrt{22}$ giving the standard error. In a simulation, the same combination is applied to the six cell values of one draw, and the values obtained across draws are not averaged. The delay-period log-rate slope is estimated on the bins of the window by the offset-Poisson regression the data are fitted with.}

\new{Two quantities are reported for each effect. The first is the range of magnitudes the model can produce: the distribution over its admissible draws, summarised by the median and the 5th to 95th percentile, against which the observed value and its between-participant confidence interval are read. The second is the fraction of draws that reproduce the observed sign. Each draw is classified, at two Monte-Carlo standard errors, as producing the effect significantly of the observed sign, significantly against it, or not distinguishably from zero; the Monte-Carlo standard error is propagated from the per-cell errors of the quantities entering the effect (binomial for accuracy, Poisson for the two activation rates), and the simulated between-trial errors for the response-time components and the log-rate slope (with every count-based error floored at one event, so that a cell containing no events is not credited with zero uncertainty). The third class is the set of draws at which the model makes no directional claim; on one effect it holds the majority.}


\section{Results}

\new{The first subsection below is devoted to behavioral results. Each subsequent subsection  takes one analysis window. All are organised the same way: it states what the two models predict there, and tests that prediction against the data. The predictions are derived as they are stated, with the heavier proofs collected in the Appendix, and the range of effect sizes each model can produce is taken from the parameter-space evaluation described in Materials and Methods.}


\subsection{Behavior}


\subsubsection{\new{Theoretical predictions}}

\new{The two models differ in one term, the delay-period temporal preparation of magnitude $\beta$, which enters through $\delta ={t}_{RS}-{t}_{offset}=3+{L}_{RS}-{L}_{motion}$. Stimulus duration cancels, so temporal preparation adds the same amount in all six coherence $\times$ duration cells and can generate no effect of coherence, of duration, or of their interaction. Every prediction below therefore holds in both models, with the same sign and the same ordering, and behavior constrains the two-stage accumulation common to both.}

\new{The choice is the sign of $x$ when the first accumulation stops. By Equation~\eqref{eq:x} its drift is $skc$, so accuracy rises with coherence and with the time for which \eqref{eq:x} runs, hence with duration. The duration benefit is confined to intermediate coherence: at $c=0$ the drift vanishes and accuracy is 50\% whatever the duration, and at the highest coherence the threshold $\theta$ is typically reached within the short stimulus, so accumulation stops before the extra time can be used.}

\new{After the response signal the second accumulation runs from the maintained level $m$ to the response criterion, at the drift ${\mu }_{\mathrm{RS}}$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:q}, which does not depend on the condition, so coherence and duration act on response time only through $m$. By Equation~\eqref{eq:m}, $m$ is ${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}\theta$ on trials that reached the threshold and $y\left({L}_{\mathrm{motion}}+D\right)$ on the rest; both branches rise with coherence and the second rises with duration, so mean response time falls with either. The first branch is a cap, and it is what makes the coherence effect on response time larger after the short stimulus: after a long stimulus most trials sit at $\theta$ at every coherence, so $m$ barely varies with coherence, whereas after a short one fewer trials reach $\theta$ and $m$ still does.}


\subsubsection{\new{Empirical results}}

Figures~\ref{fig:behavior}A and~\ref{fig:behavior}B show response accuracy and mean RT, respectively, as a function of motion coherence and duration\new{; the full ANOVA results for the four behavioral measures are in Table~\ref{tab:behav}}. Accuracy decreased as motion coherence decreased \new{(F(1.31,27.4) = 222.22, p < .001, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.914)} and was higher in the 2 s condition than in the 0.3 s condition \new{(F(1,21) = 22.68, p < .001, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.519)}, but this duration benefit was observed only at the intermediate 12\% coherence level, yielding a significant motion coherence $\times$ duration interaction  \new{(F(1.96,41.24) = 12.17, p < .001, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.367)}. Mean RT increased as motion coherence and duration decreased \new{(coherence: F(1.5,31.39) = 14.19, p < .001, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.403; duration: F(1,21) = 65.79, p < .001, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.758)}, with the effect of coherence more pronounced in the 0.3 s condition, also yielding a significant motion coherence $\times$ duration interaction  \new{(F(1.68,35.25) = 4.61, p = .022, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.180)}.


\subsubsection{\new{Simulation validation}}

\new{The parameter-space evaluation gives the range of magnitudes each model can produce for each behavioral effect (Figure~\ref{fig:effects}A--C). The duration benefit on accuracy, measured as the benefit at 12\% minus the mean of the benefits at 0\% and 50\%, was 0.075 $\pm$ 0.014 in the data, against ranges of 0.012 to 0.197 for Model 1 and 0.020 to 0.208 for Model 2. The duration effect on response time was 49 $\pm$ 6 ms, against 2 to 86 ms and 1 to 92 ms; the coherence $\times$ duration interaction on it (the coherence effect after the short stimulus minus the coherence effect after the long one, as predicted above) was +15 $\pm$ 8 ms, against 1 to 82 ms and 1 to 76 ms.} \new{Each of these effects is significantly of the observed sign at 89\% or more of each model's admissible draws and} \new{significantly against it at 3\% or fewer.} \new{On every behavioral effect the observed value falls inside both models' ranges, and the two ranges are barely distinguishable from one another, which is the quantitative form of the same point: $\beta$ cannot move any of these quantities, so no behavioral measure separates the models at any parameter setting. The ranges are wide because they describe what a model can do across its whole admissible space.}

\subsection{\new{Stimulus-locked}}

\subsubsection{\new{Theoretical predictions}}

\new{By Equation~\eqref{eq:x} the first-stage drift is $skc$, so a higher coherence carries $x$, and through Equation~\eqref{eq:y} the motor-preparatory signal $y$, further and faster. A partial activation requires that signal to exceed the gate $g$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:z}), so the partial-activation rate is predicted to rise more steeply, and to reach a higher level, at higher coherence. Neither \eqref{eq:x} nor \eqref{eq:y} involves $\beta$, so this prediction is common to both models and cannot separate them.}

\new{By duration the two models diverge, and this is the only place outside the delay period where they do. Because temporal preparation begins at stimulus offset, it occupies about three-quarters of the 2-s stimulus-onset window in the 0.3-s condition and none of it in the 2-s condition. Model 1 has no temporal preparation, so the only duration dependence left to it is in the first accumulation: the long stimulus keeps adding both evidence and variance to the motor-preparatory signal, whereas after the short stimulus the signal is maintained and its fluctuation saturates. Both push the rate up after the long stimulus, and the second operates even at zero coherence (Appendix, Proposition~\ref{prop:duration}): it predicts a higher rate after the \emph{long} stimulus. Model 2's temporal preparation pushes the effect the other way, but whether it overturns the level effect depends on its size relative to the distance to the gate, and those are independent parameters; the reversal therefore holds over part of Model 2's admissible space and not all of it. A rate higher after the \emph{short} stimulus is therefore incompatible with Model 1 and compatible with Model 2.}


\subsubsection{\new{Empirical results}}


\paragraph{\new{Partial muscle activation (PMA) rate}}

\new{As explained in Materials and Methods, because gating inhibition remained active throughout the trial, a PMA (muscle activity that failed to reach the response criterion)} \new{provides a peripheral readout of the motor-preparatory signal's position and variability relative to the gating and response criteria.} For each period of interest, Figure~\ref{fig:pmaeeg}A shows the average number of PMAs per 0.1 s time bin in the response-associated EMG channel, whereas Figure~\ref{fig:pmaeeg}B shows the corresponding data for the opposite EMG channel. As expected, PMA rates in the opposite EMG channel were near zero across conditions and periods. Consequently, subsequent analyses were restricted to the response-associated EMG channel. An ANOVA on the stimulus period data (0--2 s window relative to stimulus onset) revealed a significant time bin $\times$ motion coherence interaction \new{(linear-trend equivalent:} \new{F(1.72, 36.20) = 6.145, p = .007}\new{, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.226; full decomposition in Table~\ref{tab:pma})}. PMA rate increased over time, and the slope of this ramping activity became shallower as motion coherence decreased, consistent with an evidence accumulation mechanism. The analysis also showed a significant time bin $\times$  duration interaction \new{(linear-trend equivalent: F(1,21) = 9.532, p = .006, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.312; full decomposition in Table~\ref{tab:pma})}, reflecting steeper ramping in the 0.3 s than in the 2 s duration condition. \new{PMA rate was correspondingly higher overall in the 0.3 s than the 2 s condition across this window (main effect of duration: F(1,21) = 13.38, p = .001, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.389), the average counterpart of this interaction.} This effect is unlikely to reflect greater allocation of attentional resources in the 0.3 s condition, because stimulus duration was unpredictable at trial onset.


\paragraph{\new{Mu--beta lateralization (MBL)}}

Figure~\ref{fig:pmaeeg}D shows the MBL amplitude for each condition and period. The ANOVA conducted on the stimulus-period data reveal\new{ed a significant interaction between time bin and motion coherence, but this reflects the measure's non-monotone shape rather than a uniform coherence-dependent ramp: the full-window linear trend $\times$ coherence is not significant}  (\new{F(1.95, 40.99) = 0.517, p = .596}, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.024; Table~\ref{tab:mbl}). MBL \new{exhibited ramping activity that was non-monotone across the stimulus period: an initial buildup rate that scaled with motion coherence was confined to approximately the first second of stimulation, and this coherence dependence was extinguished over the following second, as coherence-dependent trajectories converged toward a common sustained level (Table~\ref{tab:split}), consistent with evidence accumulation toward an internal threshold rather than an unbounded coherence-dependent ramp.}


\paragraph{\new{Lateralized readiness potential (LRP)}}

Figure~\ref{fig:pmaeeg}C shows the LRP for each condition and period. Although the LRP showed a slight numerical trend suggestive of evidence accumulation during the stimulus period, the ANOVA did not reveal any significant effect \new{(linear-trend equivalent:} \new{F(1.49, 31.27) = 0.551, p = .531}\new{, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.026; Table~\ref{tab:lrp})}.


\subsubsection{\new{Simulation validation}}

\new{Two quantities from this window were evaluated over the parameter space: the coherence effect, which both models predict and which therefore serves as a control (Figure~\ref{fig:effects}D), and the duration effect, on which they disagree (Figure~\ref{fig:effects}E).}

\new{Take the control first. The shared architecture predicts} \new{the stimulus-period partial-activation rate to be higher at high coherence in both models, and it is: the rate at 50\% coherence minus the rate at 0\% was +0.054 $\pm$ 0.020 s\textsuperscript{-1} in the data, against 0.009 to 0.651 s\textsuperscript{-1} over Model 1's admissible draws and 0.001 to 0.388 over Model 2's, with the observed sign significant at 98\% and 93\% of them and the opposite sign at none.}

\new{The duration effect on the stimulus-period partial-activation rate (the rate after the 0.3 s stimulus minus the rate after the 2 s stimulus, over the same 0 to 2 s window used for the data) was +0.057 $\pm$ 0.015 s\textsuperscript{-1}. Model 1 produces a significantly negative effect at 95\% of its admissible draws and a significantly positive one at none (5th to 95th percentile $-$0.324 to $-$0.004 s\textsuperscript{-1}), which is what Model 1 predicts and what makes the observation incompatible with it. Model 2 produces the observed sign at 39\% of its draws and the opposite sign at 45\% ($-$0.214 to +0.036 s\textsuperscript{-1}): temporal preparation opposes the level effect, as the derivation says, but does not generally overturn it,} \new{because the size of temporal preparation and the distance to the gate are independent parameters. Its largest value, +0.129 s\textsuperscript{-1}, is more than twice the observation, so Model 2 can reach the observation even though most of its admissible space does not.} \new{The test is therefore one-sided. It refutes Model 1; it does not by itself confirm Model 2.}


\subsection{\new{Delay-locked}}


\subsubsection{\new{Theoretical predictions}}

\new{During the delay, a partial activation on the responding side is an upcrossing, by the fluctuation $H$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:Hdef}, of the distance from the maintained level to the gate. The Appendix derives the resulting instantaneous rate in closed form from the Kac--Rice formula for the expected upcrossing rate of a differentiable Gaussian process, Equation \eqref{eq:nu2}, which does not assume stationarity. Write} \new{${\sigma }_{H}$} \new{and} \new{${\sigma }_{\dot{H}}$} \new{for the standard deviations of} \new{$H$} \new{and of its time derivative,} \new{$\omega :=\sqrt{\lambda \kappa }$} \new{for the characteristic frequency of the fluctuation, and}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{equation}
a\left(t\right)=\frac{g-\left|m\right|-\dfrac{\beta }{\delta }{\left(t-{t}_{\mathrm{offset}}\right)}^{+}}{{\sigma }_{H}}
\label{eq:a}
\end{equation}
}

\new{for the distance from the maintained level to the gate in units of} \new{${\sigma }_{H}$}\new{, which temporal preparation shortens at the constant speed} \new{$\beta /\delta $}\new{. Where the fluctuation has reached stationarity the rate is Equation \eqref{eq:nu_asymp}:}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{equation}
\nu \left(t\right)=\varphi \left(a\left(t\right)\right)\left[\frac{\beta }{\delta {\sigma }_{H}}\Phi \left(\frac{\beta }{\delta {\sigma }_{\dot{H}}}\right)+\omega \varphi \left(\frac{\beta }{\delta {\sigma }_{\dot{H}}}\right)\right]
\label{eq:nu_asymp}
\end{equation}
}

\new{Setting} \new{$\beta =0$} \new{(Model 1) recovers the classical Rice formula, Equation \eqref{eq:nu1}, in which the rate falls as a Gaussian in the standardized distance to the gate:}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{equation}
\nu =\frac{\omega }{2\pi }{e}^{-{a}^{2}/2}
\label{eq:nu1}
\end{equation}
}

\new{Three properties of the delay-period rate follow: whether it rises over the window, the shape of that rise, and how its level differs across cells. By \emph{rise} we mean the increase of the partial-activation rate itself, not the temporal preparation that may or may not drive it.}

\new{Whether the rate rises follows from Equation~\eqref{eq:a}. Under Model 1 temporal preparation is absent, so the numerator of} \new{$a$} \new{is the constant} \new{$g-\left|m\right|$}\new{; once the fluctuation has reached stationarity the denominator is constant as well, and \eqref{eq:nu1} then gives a rate constant in time. In that regime Model 1 predicts no rise at all. Under Model 2 the numerator falls at the constant speed} \new{$\beta /\delta $}\new{, so} \new{$a$} \new{falls linearly and the rate rises.}

\new{Taking logarithms in Equation \eqref{eq:nu_asymp} (Appendix, Corollary~\ref{cor:logslope}) gives the rise under Model 2, Equation \eqref{eq:slope}:}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd \left(\ln \nu \right)}{\dd \rho }=\frac{\beta }{\delta {\sigma }_{H}}a\left(\rho \right), \frac{{\dd }^{2}\left(\ln \nu \right)}{\dd {\rho }^{2}}=-{\left(\frac{\beta }{\delta {\sigma }_{H}}\right)}^{2}
\label{eq:slope}
\end{equation}
}

\new{The log rate rises at a rate proportional to the current standardized distance to the gate, so a cell with a higher rate has a smaller standardized distance and therefore a shallower predicted slope.}

\new{Model 1 can rise only before stationarity, while} \new{${\sigma }_{H}\left(t\right)$} \new{is still growing and} \new{$a$} \new{therefore still falling. That is the whole of its delay-period rise, and it is bought at a price: a rise requires the signal to stand more than one fluctuation standard deviation from the gate, and by \eqref{eq:nu1} each further standard deviation of distance costs a factor} \new{${e}^{-{a}^{2}/2}$} \new{in the rate itself. Rise and rate are governed by the same quantity in opposite directions (Corollary~\ref{cor:tradeoff}), so the question is not whether Model 1 can produce either but whether it can produce both at once.}

\new{The two models differ in the shape of the rise as well. Under Model 2 the curvature in \eqref{eq:slope} is the negative of the square of the speed of temporal preparation in units of} \new{${\sigma }_{H}$}\new{: it depends on the parameters, but it is constant in time, so the log rate is exactly a downward parabola, and it is the same parabola in all six conditions, since temporal preparation and the fluctuation are properties of the participant and not of the condition. Under Model 1 the rise is carried by the filling-in of the variance, which decelerates: by Corollary~\ref{cor:decay} the local log-rate slope must fall by at least a factor of 3.6 across the analyzed window whatever} \new{$\kappa$}\new{, and by a factor of at least 15 everywhere in the sampled ranges.}

\new{The level of the rate across cells follows from the cap on the maintained target. That target is capped pathwise at the internal threshold} \new{$\theta$}\new{: a trial whose first accumulation reaches} \new{$\theta$} \new{has its target pinned there for the rest of the delay, though its signal keeps fluctuating about that target, which is what produces the crossings in the first place. On such a trial the numerator of \eqref{eq:a} is} \new{$g-\theta$} \new{in every condition, and the law of} \new{$H$} \new{does not depend on coherence either, so the rate is coherence-independent. Writing} \new{$F\left(D,c\right)$} \new{for the fraction of trials at duration} \new{$D$} \new{and coherence} \new{$c$} \new{whose target has reached} \new{$\theta$}\new{, the coherence levels converge as} \new{$F\left(D,c\right)\to 1$}\new{, and a longer stimulus raises} \new{$F$} \new{at every coherence. The coherence effect on the delay-period rate should therefore shrink after a long stimulus, not grow.}

\new{The prediction is conditional: the cap shrinks a difference only once both of its terms are capped, so it requires the \emph{lowest}-coherence long-duration cell to be near the cap, and reverses where that fails, since below the cap the target is free and its coherence sensitivity grows with duration.}


\subsubsection{\new{Empirical results}}


\paragraph{\new{Partial muscle activation (PMA) rate}}

\new{Figure~\ref{fig:pmaeeg}A shows that PMA rate continued to increase during the delay period. This increase is best attributed to temporal preparation: the time bin $\times$ motion coherence interaction, a diagnostic marker of evidence accumulation, was no longer significant in this window  (linear-trend equivalent:} \new{F(1.70, 35.61) = 0.524, p = .567}\new{, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.024; Table~\ref{tab:pma}) and did not interact with duration  (linear-trend equivalent:} \new{F(1.74, 36.45) = 0.508, p = .580}\new{, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.024; Table~\ref{tab:pma}), whereas the main effect of time bin remained significant  (linear-trend equivalent: F(1,21) = 35.238, p < .001, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.627; Table~\ref{tab:pma}), indicating continued ramping. An unbinned, point-process analysis of exact activation onset times confirms this ramp directly (Table~\ref{tab:pp}): the log-rate slope was significantly positive (two-stage F(1,21) = 102.95, p < .001) and shallower after a long stimulus and at high coherence,} \new{both expected under accumulation to an internal threshold. The two analyses measure different quantities: the trend contrast above asks whether the raw rate ramps at a coherence-dependent rate, and it does not, whereas the point-process estimate asks whether its logarithm does, which} \new{Equation \eqref{eq:slope}} \new{requires that it should: a cell with a higher rate has by construction a smaller standardized distance to the gate and therefore a shallower log-rate slope. The residual coherence dependence of the log-rate slope accordingly runs in the direction saturation predicts, shallower at high coherence, and not in the direction continued motion-evidence accumulation would predict, so neither test implies that accumulation is still under way during the delay. Model 1,} \new{which has no temporal preparation, cannot account for this pattern alone. }

\new{A significant time bin $\times$ duration interaction was also observed: PMA rate was generally higher in the 2 s than in the 0.3 s condition (F(1,21) = 33.19, p < .001, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.612), and this difference increased over time  (linear-trend equivalent: F(1,21) = 11.898, p = .002, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.362; Table~\ref{tab:pma}), the reverse of the ordering seen during the stimulus period, consistent with the different temporal alignment of the two windows (evidence accumulation has barely started by stimulus offset in the 0.3 s condition, but has typically already reached threshold in the 2 s condition). }

\new{Finally, a duration $\times$ motion coherence interaction (}\new{F(1.92, 40.42) = 3.474, p = .042}\new{, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.142) showed a larger coherence effect on delay-period PMA rate after the 2 s than after the 0.3 s stimulus,} \new{the opposite of what the model predicts (Theoretical predictions, above), and the opposite direction} \new{to the coherence effect on mean PMET, where the 0.3 s condition showed the larger effect. Two candidate explanations for this reversal, selective exclusion of the most prepared high-coherence trials via anticipatory-response removal and a strategic reduction of temporal preparation as motor preparation approached the gate, are both unlikely to hold: the anticipatory-response rate was too low to account for the size of the reversal (Figure~\ref{fig:premature}), and the model shows that the apparent leveling-off of the rate late in the 2 s, high-coherence condition requires no strategic adjustment, since a higher rate corresponds by construction to a smaller distance to the gate and therefore} \new{a shallower log-rate slope (}\new{Equation \eqref{eq:slope}}\new{, above). We therefore withdraw} \new{the strategic-reduction account and instead report the coherence $\times$ duration interaction on delay-period PMA rate as a genuine point of disagreement between the model and the data (see Discussion),} \new{not a failure of parameter choice that a different setting would resolve}\new{: because the maintained level is capped at the internal threshold $\theta$, the model predicts a coherence effect on delay-period rate that shrinks, not grows, after a long stimulus, at every parameter setting} \new{compatible with the observed level of decision accuracy (Theoretical predictions, above).}

\new{Figure~\ref{fig:shapedata} shows this relation in the data alone}\new{: the six delay-period log-rate profiles (left) and, across the six stimulus-duration $\times$ coherence cells, the negative relation between a cell's rate and its log-rate slope (right), as} \new{Equation \eqref{eq:slope}} \new{requires.}


\paragraph{\new{Mu--beta lateralization (MBL)}}

Unlike the PMA rate, MBL amplitude showed a slight decrease before transitioning to sustained activity, at least in the highest coherence conditions. During the delay, sustained MBL activity increased over time \new{(main effect of time bin;  [linear-trend equivalent: F(1,21) = 7.107, p = .014, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.253; Table~\ref{tab:mbl}]}), suggesting that MBL also reflects temporal preparation. In contrast to the PMA rate, none of the effects of motion coherence or duration reached statistical significance \new{(coherence: F(1.29,27.12) = 1.257, p = .285; duration: F(1,21) = 3.177, p = .089)}. \new{As for the LRP, this coherence-independent delay-period ramp discriminates the two models: Model 1 predicts no systematic change in MBL amplitude across the delay, whereas Model 2 predicts the sustained increase observed here.}


\paragraph{\new{Lateralized readiness potential (LRP)}}

During the delay, however, the analysis showed a clear effect of time bin \new{(linear-trend equivalent: F(1,21) = 14.338, p = .001, $\eta$p\textsuperscript{2} = 0.406; Table~\ref{tab:lrp})}: the LRP became progressively more negative, consistent with temporal preparation for the upcoming response. \new{ This coherence-independent delay-period ramp is itself diagnostic between the two models: Model 1, which has no temporal-preparation process, predicts a flat LRP throughout the delay, whereas Model 2 predicts the progressive ramp observed here.} These findings are partially consistent with our hypothesis. Although the LRP primarily reflects the final stage of motor preparation, it also indexes temporal preparation during the delay for the selected response, suggesting that the underlying neural circuits are informed about the outcome of the motion evidence accumulation process.


\subsubsection{\new{Simulation validation}}


\paragraph{\new{Model 2, not Model 1, predicts the observed delay-period ramp}}

\new{Behavior left the two models nearly indistinguishable (Behavior, above), as the shared architecture implies it must. The delay-period rate profile is where they separate.}

\new{The trade-off can be computed exactly, as the largest log-rate slope Model 1 can produce at each level of the delay-period rate,} \new{maximizing over the distance to the gate and over the ranges of $\kappa$ and $\lambda$ in Table~\ref{tab:params}, computing the moments from the kernel rather than from their stationary limits, and estimating the slope on the bins of the window by the same offset-Poisson regression used on the data (Materials and Methods).}

\new{Two properties make it a bound. The first is that it survives the trial-to-trial variation of the maintained level. Within a participant $\kappa$, $\lambda$ and ${\sigma }_{H}$ are common to every trial, but the level held is the internal threshold $\theta$ on trials that reached it and $y\left({t}_{\mathrm{offset}}\right)$ on the others, so the measured rate is a mixture over the distance to the gate. Both the mixture's rate at the end of the window and its rate at the start are linear in the mixing weights, so maximizing the slope at a fixed end-of-window rate is a linear programme in those weights: with two equality constraints the optimum carries at most two non-zero weights, and the bound is the lower convex envelope of the curve the single-distance rates trace out. It moves the frontier by less than one per cent. The second is that the two effects the calculation does not model (the merging of nearby crossings into one detected activation, and the absorption of trials that reach the response criterion) both act to lower the measured slope, not to raise it (Appendix, Remark~\ref{rem:scope}).}  \new{At the lowest rate observed in any participant $\times$ condition cell, 0.085 s\textsuperscript{-1}, Model 1 can reach a slope of +0.81 s\textsuperscript{-1}; at 0.2 s\textsuperscript{-1} it can reach +0.40; at 0.3 s\textsuperscript{-1}, +0.19; at 0.4 s\textsuperscript{-1}, +0.05; at 0.5 s\textsuperscript{-1}, +0.01; and at the observed mean rate of 0.709 s\textsuperscript{-1} it can reach nothing distinguishable from zero.}  \new{The observed slope is +0.89 s\textsuperscript{-1} at that rate, the mean of the per-cell, per-participant slopes, which is the quantity a simulated homogeneous process must be compared with; the slope of the profile pooled across participants is flatter (+0.82) because pooling mixes processes at different distances from the gate.} \new{The largest rate at which Model 1 attains the observed slope anywhere in the sampled ranges is 0.069 s\textsuperscript{-1}, a tenth of the observed mean, and below the 0.085 s\textsuperscript{-1} of the least active participant in the least active condition. There is therefore no admissible setting of its parameters at which Model 1 produces both the observed ramp and partial activations as often as any participant produced them.} \new{Because the optimum lies at the smallest $\kappa$ and the largest $\lambda$ the sampled ranges allow, this frontier is the most favorable one available to the model.}

\new{The same calculation applies to Model 2. Temporal preparation moves the gate-facing signal towards the gate, which enters the crossing-rate formula in one place only, the derivative of the signal relative to the barrier, so the frontier can be computed for that model too. That calculation knows nothing of admissibility, and unbounded temporal preparation would give an unbounded slope, so it has to be capped by hand. Capping it at three fluctuation standard deviations over the analyzed window (near the 97th percentile of the temporal-preparation magnitudes the admissibility screen accepts, whose median is 0.39 and whose maximum is 8.4, so that 4\% of admissible draws exceed the cap),} \new{Model 2 reaches a delay-period log-rate slope of +4.05 s\textsuperscript{-1} at the observed rate, against an observed +0.89; and it attains the observed slope at rates up to 6.4 s\textsuperscript{-1}, nine times the observed rate. Model 1 at the same rate reaches nothing distinguishable from zero, and attains the observed slope only at 0.069 s\textsuperscript{-1}.} \new{Model 2 exceeds what has to be explained several times over; Model 1 cannot reach it at any rate a participant showed.} \new{This is also why the two frontiers have a different status. For Model 1 the frontier is a bound: with no temporal preparation the rate depends on nothing but $\kappa$, $\lambda$ and the distance to the gate, all three of which are swept, and nothing outside the calculation can raise it. For Model 2 it is an upper envelope under a cap read off the screened sample, so it says what that model can reach, not what it typically does.}

\new{This is the one window in which the two models' ranges separate cleanly (Figure~\ref{fig:effects}I). Two quantities have to be reproduced together here: the rate at which partial activations occur during the delay, and the slope of its logarithm. Over the admissible draws Model 1's slope is centred near zero (median $-$0.02 s\textsuperscript{-1}, 5th to 95th percentile $-$0.20 to +0.09) and Model 2's is positive (median +0.27, $-$0.06 to +1.98). Classified against their Monte-Carlo error, Model 1's slope is significantly positive at 3\% of its draws, significantly negative at 39\%, and indistinguishable from zero at the remaining 58\%; Model 2's is significantly positive at 81\%, negative at 5\%, and flat at 14\%. Model 1 produces a flat rate, which is what a model with no temporal preparation should produce and what the data contradict. Model 2 produces all six cell slopes significantly positive in 76 of its 148 admissible draws while Model 1 does so in none of its 164. Requiring both quantities at once is decisive, and is what the frontier above predicts. No draw of Model 1 reaches the observed slope range at any rate; 16 draws of Model 2 do.}

\new{Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} shows this directly. The two quantities are on the two axes, the frontier is the boundary of what Model 1 can produce, and the observation lies far outside it. Two features of the picture do not read off it directly. Model 1's draws lie along the bottom of the plot at every rate, which is the frontier holding. \subsubsection{\new{Theoretical predictions}}

\new{By Equation~\eqref{eq:m} the maintained level $m$ is the only quantity the conditions move, and it sets where the second accumulation starts. Both models therefore predict an increase in mean response time} \new{following reductions in stimulus discriminability or duration, carried entirely by mean pre-motor execution time (PMET), the interval from RS onset to muscle-activation onset, with mean motor execution time (MET) unchanged (Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamics}). \section{Discussion}

A central question for evidence accumulation models is how a decision is preserved when commitment and action are separated in time. A simple possibility is that the choice formed during stimulus presentation is stored and released when action is permitted. The present findings argue against this passive storage-and-release view. Replicating previous work \parencites{achardExtendingContinuousFlow2025}{MotorCorticalSignals}, mean RT increased as motion coherence or duration decreased, even though RT was measured from RS onset, 3 s after stimulus offset. Delayed report latency therefore remained tied to prior motion evidence.

\new{This study identifies the motor-preparatory mechanisms underlying that effect. EMG gave single-trial access to two events: PMAs, a time-resolved proxy of motor-preparatory activity, and the onset of response-related muscle activation, which fractionates RT into PMET and MET }\new{\parencites{colesPsychophysiologicalInvestigationContinuous}{servantIntegratedTheoryDeciding2021}. PMAs were nearly absent in the opposite channel but clearly expressed in the response-associated one, indicating effector-selective preparation rather than nonspecific muscular noise. PMA rate increased during stimulus presentation with a buildup rate that scaled with motion coherence, consistent with evidence accumulation at the motor-preparatory level, as also described in non-human primate reaching decisions \parencites{cisekNeuralCorrelatesReaching2005}{donnerBuildupChoicePredictiveActivity2009}{kellyInternalExternalInfluences2013}{MotorCorticalSignals}{selenDeliberationMotorSystem2012}; remained elevated throughout the delay, indicating a sustained motor preparatory state; and decreased after RS onset, consistent with a second accumulation phase that adds drive to this state and pushes activity toward an overt response rather than remaining partial. These observations support the core proposal that sensory evidence is accumulated and transmitted to motor circuits, where it is maintained until action is permitted. This is the empirical counterpart of the mouse/primate correspondence anticipated in the Introduction \parencites{inagakiNeuralAlgorithmsCircuits2022}{churchlandPreparatoryActivityExpansive2024}: a selective maintained state, separable in its dynamics and at RS onset from the non-selective drive that culminates in the response.}

RT fractionation then linked this preparatory state to delayed report latency, explaining why motion coherence and duration modulate mean RT. Within the theory, PMET indexes the starting point of the second accumulation phase relative to gating inhibition, whereas MET is the time from muscle-activation onset to response. That coherence and duration modulated mean PMET but not mean MET localizes the effects to processes preceding muscle-activation onset, and therefore to the starting point of the second accumulation phase. \new{Both models predict this identically: temporal preparation is anchored to stimulus offset and the RS follows at a fixed interval, so it contributes the same amount in every condition (Results). Behavior therefore constrains the two-stage architecture the models share, and does not choose between them.}

The critical question is therefore why this starting point varied across conditions. \new{Two windows of the PMA rate bear on it. During stimulus presentation the rate was higher after the short than after the long stimulus (+0.057 $\pm$ 0.015 s\textsuperscript{-1}). Model 1 has no temporal preparation, so its only duration dependence is the extra evidence the long stimulus has accumulated by the end of the window; it gives the opposite sign at 95\% of its admissible draws and the observed sign at none.} \new{During the delay, PMA rate ramped throughout, without the coherence-dependent buildup rate seen during the stimulus. Model 1 does produce a delay-period ramp, as the variance of the maintenance fluctuation fills in, but it cannot produce one of the observed steepness while partial activations also occur as often as they do (below); temporal preparation for the RS produces both,} in line with work on anticipatory motor activity \parencites{burleExcitatoryInhibitoryMotor2010}{heidemanTemporalAlignmentAnticipatory2018}{ramayyaHumanResponseTimes2025}. Motion duration also affected accuracy, not just latency: accuracy was higher in the 2 s than the 0.3 s condition at intermediate coherence, so longer stimuli improved the evidence-dependent state itself, not merely the time available for temporal preparation. The coherence $\times$ duration interaction on mean PMET is convergent evidence for this joint contribution.

\new{The coherence $\times$ duration interaction on delay-period PMA rate (larger after the 2 s than the 0.3 s stimulus) further specifies how duration shaped the state inherited from the first phase. A longer stimulus lets more trials reach the internal threshold and pin there \parencites{mazurekRoleNeuralIntegrators2003}{kianiBoundedIntegrationParietal2008}, which is why the maintained level's coherence-related differences shrink after a 2-s stimulus, as the account of PMET and RT above requires (Results). The same logic predicts the coherence effect on delay-period PMA rate should shrink too, not grow.} \new{The parameter-space evaluation bears this out: the effect runs in the observed direction at 4\% of Model 1's admissible draws and 7\% of Model 2's, and significantly against it at 85\% and 87\% (Results). We therefore treat this interaction as a model limitation, and no longer read it as evidence for Model 2 over Model 1.}

The EEG results complement this picture. MBL tracked motion-evidence accumulation early in the stimulus period, with a coherence-dependent buildup rate confined to the first second before saturating; MBL then remained sustained during the delay while continuing to increase, consistent with alpha-/beta-band lateralization carrying decision-related information into motor cortex before response implementation \parencites{MotorCorticalSignals}{twomeyAbstractEffectorSelectiveDecision2016}, and refined here: that sustained state is also shaped by temporal preparation for the expected RS. The LRP did not reliably track motion-evidence accumulation, but ramped during the delay, continued after RS onset, and peaked near the response: temporal preparation was not confined to upstream representations but also shaped later-stage effector-selective preparation.

This hierarchy explains why PMA rate was the most sensitive readout of motor preparation here. MBL captured evidence-dependent buildup and delay-period temporal preparation; the LRP captured temporal preparation and later-stage motor preparation; neither revealed the full coherence- and duration-dependent structure PMA rate did across the stimulus, delay and post-RS periods, likely because PMAs are single-trial events measured directly from muscle. PMA rate is therefore a promising tool for studying how decision evidence, temporal expectation, urgency, inhibitory control, and action constraints jointly shape motor preparation \parencites{balsdonSecondaryMotorIntegration2023}{dendauwGatedCascadeDiffusion2024}{ramayyaHumanResponseTimes2025}{MotorCorticalSignals}{servantIntegratedTheoryDeciding2021}, and a natural next step is to use it to constrain formal models of threshold-reaching probability and temporal preparation.

\new{Two features of the modeling should be kept in view. No parameter is estimated from these data: the predictions tested are ordinal and derived analytically (Appendix), and the parameter-space evaluation checks them over the ranges of Table~\ref{tab:params} rather than at a single point. No statement here is about any participant's parameters, and no parameter value is interpreted. What the evaluation establishes is a difference in what the two models can produce at all. Over the admissible space, the model without temporal preparation cannot generate a delay-period ramp of the observed size while also generating partial activations at the observed rate; the closed-form frontier of the Appendix shows why, the two being governed by the same quantity in opposite directions. Nor can it generate the observed shape: without temporal preparation the rise is carried by the filling-in of the maintenance variance, which decelerates by at least a factor of 15 across the analyzed window everywhere in the sampled ranges, whereas the observed log-rate slope is as steep at the end of the delay as at the start. PMA rate was the only measure that separated the two models quantitatively; MBL and LRP separate them only by the presence or absence of a delay-period ramp.}

\new{A scope condition concerns the coherence range: we sampled three levels (0\%, 12\%, and 50\%), coarser than the five- or six-level logarithmic series more commonly used to characterize psychometric and chronometric functions in random-dot motion studies \parencites[e.g., 3.2--51.2\%][]{roitmanResponseNeuronsLateral2002}[6.4--51.2\%][]{kianiBoundedIntegrationParietal2008}. This was sufficient to establish the coherence-dependent effects reported here, though not their full functional form. A second concerns delay length, fixed here at 3 s: companion data \parencite{achardExtendingContinuousFlow2025} show the same build-up preserved across 3--7 s delays when the response mapping is foreknown, but largely absent when revealed only at the RS.}

In conclusion, delayed decision reports are not generated by storing an abstract completed choice and releasing it on demand. Instead, prior sensory evidence remains embodied in motor preparatory circuits, where it is maintained, reshaped by temporal expectations, and converted into overt action when the appropriate signal appears. By combining RT fractionation, PMA dynamics, and EEG markers of cortical motor preparation, the present study shows how evidence accumulation can bridge perception, decision maintenance, and action, reframing motor preparation as an active substrate of decision maintenance rather than a passive buffer.

\section{\new{Appendix. The rate of partial muscle activations}}

\new{This appendix derives the instantaneous rate at which the maintained motor-preparatory signal crosses the gate upward (a partial muscle activation) and the structural properties of that rate which the Results test. It contains no numerical evaluation: every quantity computed over the sampled parameter space is reported in Results, beside the data it is compared with.}

\new{\textbf{\emph{Setup and notation}}}

\new{Notation follows Theory (Materials and Methods) and Table~\ref{tab:params};} \new{$\varphi$} \new{and} \new{$\Phi$} \new{are the standard normal density and distribution function. Fix a trial that has entered maintenance at time} \new{$\tau $} \new{with selected response} \new{${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}$} \new{and maintained level} \new{$m$} \new{(Equation \eqref{eq:m}), and let} \new{$\rho :=t-\tau $} \new{for elapsed maintenance time. For} \new{$t\ge \tau $} \new{let} \new{$y\left(t\right)=m+H\left(t\right)$}\new{, so that} \new{$H$} \new{is the fluctuation of the motor-preparatory signal about its target, with} \new{$H\left(\tau \right)=0$}\new{. By Equations \eqref{eq:epsilon} and \eqref{eq:y_delay},} \new{$H$} \new{is the output of the first-order filter driven by the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck maintenance noise:}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{eqnarray}
    \dd \varepsilon \left(t\right) &=& -\kappa \varepsilon \left(t\right)\, \dd t+{\sigma }_{\varepsilon }\, \dd {W}_{\varepsilon }\left(t\right), \nonumber \\
    \dd H\left(t\right) &= &\lambda \left(\varepsilon \left(t\right)-H\left(t\right)\right)\, \dd t, \varepsilon \left(\tau \right)=H\left(\tau \right)=0
\label{eq:H}
\end{eqnarray}
}

\new{with} \new{${\sigma }_{\varepsilon }={\mathrm{SD}}_{\varepsilon }\sqrt{2\kappa }$}\new{, so that the stationary standard deviation of} \new{$\varepsilon$} \new{is} \new{${\mathrm{SD}}_{\varepsilon }$}.  \new{Since $\varepsilon$} \new{is symmetric and independent of the sign of the response,} \new{${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}H$} \new{has the same law as} \new{$H$}\new{. Writing} \new{$\delta :={t}_{\mathrm{RS}}-{t}_{\mathrm{offset}}$} \new{for the length of the preparation window and} \new{$d(t)$} \new{for the distance from the maintained level to the gate,}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{equation}
d\left(t\right)=g-\left|m\right|-\frac{\beta }{\delta }{\left(t-{t}_{\mathrm{offset}}\right)}^{+}
\label{eq:d}
\end{equation}
}

\new{(the second term present only under temporal preparation, so that} \new{$\beta =0$} \new{under Model 1), a partial activation on the selected channel begins exactly when} \new{${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}H$} \new{crosses} \new{$d\left(t\right)$} \new{upward. Temporal preparation therefore carries the signal toward the gate at the constant speed} \new{$\beta /\delta$}\new{. The non-selected channel activates when} \new{${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}H$} \new{falls below the corresponding distance on the far side, which is several fluctuation standard deviations further away over the whole admissible space; the model predicts that channel to be silent, as it is observed to be, and it is not considered further.}

\new{\textbf{\emph{The maintained fluctuation}}}

\new{Solving  equations  \eqref{eq:epsilon} and \eqref{eq:H} gives}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{eqnarray*}
    \varepsilon \left(\tau +\rho \right)&=&{\sigma }_{\varepsilon }\int_{0}^{\rho } {e}^{-\kappa \left(\rho -p\right)}\,\dd {W}_{\varepsilon }\left(p\right),\\ H\left(\tau +\rho \right)&=&\lambda \int_{0}^{\rho } {e}^{-\lambda \left(\rho -q\right)}\varepsilon \left(\tau +q\right)\, \dd q
\end{eqnarray*}
}

\new{Since $\varepsilon$ admits a continuous modification and $H$ is then a Riemann integral of a continuous function, almost surely $H\in C^{1}$}.
\new{ Substituting the first equation above into the second and exchanging the order of integration (permissible because the integrand is bounded on $\{0\le p\le q\le \rho \}$)} \new{lead to:}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{eqnarray*}  
    H\left(\tau +\rho \right)=\int_{0}^{\rho } K\left(\rho -p\right)\,
    \dd {W}_{\varepsilon }\left(p\right)\\
    K\left(v\right)=
    \begin{cases}
        \frac{\lambda {\sigma }_{\varepsilon }}{\lambda -\kappa }\left({e}^{-\kappa v}-{e}^{-\lambda v}\right), \text{ if } \kappa\neq \lambda, \\
\lambda {\sigma }_{\varepsilon }\,v\,{e}^{-\lambda v}, \text{ if } \kappa =\lambda.
        \end{cases}
\end{eqnarray*}
}
\new{$H$ is therefore a centred Gaussian process.} \new{Note that $K\left(0\right)=0$}\new{, which makes $K$ differentiable, and} \new{${K}^{'}\left(0\right)=\lambda {\sigma }_{\varepsilon }$}\new{.}
\new{Differentiating $H$ under the integral, and using} \new{$K\left(0\right)=0$} \new{to kill the boundary term, gives} \new{$\dot{H}\left(\tau +\rho \right)=\int_{0}^{\rho } {K}^{'}\left(\rho -p\right)\, \dd {W}_{\varepsilon }\left(p\right)$}\new{. Write, for} \new{$t\ge \tau $}\new{,} \new{${\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(t\right):=\Var H\left(t\right)$}\new{,} \new{${\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}\left(t\right):=\Var \dot{H}\left(t\right)$} \new{and} \new{${c}_{H}\left(t\right):=\Cov \left(H\left(t\right),\dot{H}\left(t\right)\right)$}\new{, with} \new{${\sigma }_{H}\left(t\right)$} \new{and} \new{${\sigma }_{\dot{H}}\left(t\right)$} \new{the corresponding standard deviations; the same symbols without} \new{time index} \new{denote their stationary limits as} \new{$t\to \infty$} \new{(Equation \eqref{eq:asymp_moments}). The It\^o isometry then gives the three moments as integrals of the kernel,} \new{${\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(t\right)=\int_{0}^{\rho } {K}^{2}$}\new{,} \new{${\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}\left(t\right)=\int_{0}^{\rho } {\left({K}^{'}\right)}^{2}$} \new{and} \new{${c}_{H}\left(t\right)=\int_{0}^{\rho } K{K}^{'}=1/2K{\left(\rho \right)}^{2}$}\new{, the last of which is half the derivative of the first. Evaluating them,}

{\color{addcol}
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:varH}
      {\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(t\right)=
  \begin{cases}    
\frac{{\lambda }^{2}{\sigma }_{\varepsilon }^{2}}{{\left(\lambda -\kappa \right)}^{2}}\left[\frac{1-{e}^{-2\kappa \rho }}{2\kappa }-\frac{2\left(1-{e}^{-\left(\lambda +\kappa \right)\rho }\right)}{\lambda +\kappa }+\frac{1-{e}^{-2\lambda \rho }}{2\lambda }\right]
, \text{ if } \kappa\neq \lambda \\
\frac{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{4\lambda}
 \left[1-e^{-2\lambda\rho}\left(1+2\lambda\rho+2\lambda^{2}\rho^{2}\right)\right],
\text{ if } \kappa =\lambda
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
}
and
{\color{addcol}
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:vardotH}
      {\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}\left(t\right)=
      \begin{cases}
          \frac{{\lambda }^{2}{\sigma }_{\varepsilon }^{2}}{{\left(\lambda -\kappa \right)}^{2}}\left[\frac{\kappa \left(1-{e}^{-2\kappa \rho }\right)}{2}-\frac{2\kappa \lambda \left(1-{e}^{-\left(\lambda +\kappa \right)\rho }\right)}{\lambda +\kappa }+\frac{\lambda \left(1-{e}^{-2\lambda \rho }\right)}{2}\right], \text{ if } \kappa\neq \lambda \\
          \frac{\lambda\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{4} \left[1-e^{-2\lambda\rho}\left(1-2\lambda\rho+2\lambda^{2}\rho^{2}\right)\right], \text{ if } \kappa = \lambda
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
}

\new{As} \new{$\rho \to \infty$} \new{both cases tend to the stationary limits:}  
{\color{addcol}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:asymp_moments}
\begin{array}{l}
    {\sigma }_{H}^{2} = \frac{\lambda \, {\mathrm{SD}}_{\varepsilon }^{2}}{\lambda +\kappa }, \\
    {\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}= \frac{{\lambda }^{2}\kappa \, {\mathrm{SD}}_{\varepsilon }^{2}}{\lambda +\kappa },\\
    {c}_{H} = 0.
\end{array}
\end{equation}
}

\new{Temporal preparation is added outside the filter (Equation \eqref{eq:tildey}), so it shifts the mean of the gate-facing signal and leaves all three moments untouched; \eqref{eq:varH} to \eqref{eq:asymp_moments} hold for both models.}

\new{\textbf{\emph{The crossing rate}}}

\new{Let} \new{$Z\left(t\right):={s}_{\mathrm{resp}}H\left(t\right)-d\left(t\right)$}\new{, so that upcrossings of} \new{$d$} \new{by} \new{${s}_{\mathrm{resp}}H$} \new{are upcrossings of zero by} \new{$Z$}\new{, and} \new{$\dot{Z}=\dot{H}+\beta /\delta$}\new{. Trajectories that reach the response criterion before the end of the delay are neglected; they are premature responses, excluded from the empirical analysis and held to a small fraction of trials by the admissibility screen. Since} \new{$H$} \new{is almost surely continuously differentiable and} \new{$\left(Z,\dot{Z}\right)$} \new{is jointly Gaussian and non-degenerate for} \new{$\rho >0$}\new{, the Kac--Rice formula gives the expected upcrossing rate \parencite[ch. 3]{azaisLevelSetsExtrema2009}:}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{equation}
\nu \left(t\right)={p}_{Z\left(t\right)}\left(0\right)\, \mathrm{E}\left[\dot{Z}\left(t\right){1}_{\{\dot{Z}\left(t\right)>0\}} | Z\left(t\right)=0\right]
\label{eq:nu}
\end{equation}
}

\new{Since} \new{$H$} \new{is centred,} \new{$Z\left(t\right)\sim \mathrm{N}\left(-d\left(t\right),{\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(t\right)\right)$} \new{and} \new{${p}_{Z\left(t\right)}\left(0\right)=\varphi \left(d\left(t\right)/{\sigma }_{H}\left(t\right)\right)/{\sigma }_{H}\left(t\right)$}\new{. Because} \new{$\left(Z,\dot{Z}\right)$} \new{is jointly Gaussian,} \new{$\dot{Z}$} \new{given} \new{$Z=0$} \new{is Gaussian with}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{equation}
M\left(t\right)=\frac{\beta }{\delta }+\frac{{c}_{H}\left(t\right)}{{\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(t\right)}d\left(t\right), S\left(t\right)={\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}\left(t\right)-\frac{{c}_{H}{\left(t\right)}^{2}}{{\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(t\right)}
\label{eq:MS}
\end{equation}
}

\new{and for} \new{$X\sim \mathrm{N}\left(M,S\right)$} \new{one has} \new{$\mathrm{E}\left[X{1}_{\{X>0\}}\right]=M\Phi \left(M/\sqrt{S}\right)+\sqrt{S}\varphi \left(M/\sqrt{S}\right)$}\new{. Substituting into \eqref{eq:nu} gives the rate in general form,}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{equation}
\nu \left(t\right)=\frac{\varphi \left(d\left(t\right)/{\sigma }_{H}\left(t\right)\right)}{{\sigma }_{H}\left(t\right)}\left[M\left(t\right)\Phi \left(\frac{M\left(t\right)}{\sqrt{S\left(t\right)}}\right)+\sqrt{S\left(t\right)}\varphi \left(\frac{M\left(t\right)}{\sqrt{S\left(t\right)}}\right)\right]
\label{eq:nu2}
\end{equation}
}

\new{Once the transient has passed the covariance vanishes, so} \new{$M=\beta /\delta$} \new{and} \new{$S={\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}$}\new{. Writing} \new{$a\left(t\right):=d\left(t\right)/{\sigma }_{H}$} \new{for the standardized distance from the maintained level to the gate, \eqref{eq:nu2} becomes}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{equation}
\nu \left(t\right)=\varphi \left(a\left(t\right)\right)\left[\frac{\beta }{\delta {\sigma }_{H}}\Phi \left(\frac{\beta }{\delta {\sigma }_{\dot{H}}}\right)+\omega \varphi \left(\frac{\beta }{\delta {\sigma }_{\dot{H}}}\right)\right]
\tag{\ref{eq:nu_asymp}}
\end{equation}
}
\new{where}
{\color{addcol}
\begin{equation*}
    \omega :=\frac{{\sigma }_{\dot{H}}}{{\sigma }_{H}}=\sqrt{\lambda \kappa }.
\end{equation*}
}
\new{Note that the bracket does not depend on the distance to the gate, and temporal preparation enters it only through} \new{$\beta /\delta$}\new{, the speed at which temporal preparation carries the signal toward the gate.}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{corollary}[the log-rate slope]\label{cor:logslope}
\new{Under Model 2 the barrier moves at constant speed, so by \eqref{eq:d} the standardized distance falls linearly,} \new{$a\left(\rho \right)=a\left(0\right)-\beta \rho /\left(\delta {\sigma }_{H}\right)$}\new{. Taking logarithms in \eqref{eq:nu_asymp}, the bracket is constant in} \new{$\rho$} \new{and} \new{$\ln \varphi \left(a\right)=-{a}^{2}/2-\ln \sqrt{2\pi }$}\new{, so}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd \left(\ln \nu \right)}{\dd \rho }=\frac{\beta }{\delta {\sigma }_{H}}a\left(\rho \right), \frac{{\dd }^{2}\left(\ln \nu \right)}{\dd {\rho }^{2}}=-{\left(\frac{\beta }{\delta {\sigma }_{H}}\right)}^{2}
\tag{\ref{eq:slope}}
\end{equation}
}

\new{The log rate rises at a rate proportional to the current standardized distance to the gate. Its curvature is negative, and constant in time (the log rate is exactly a downward parabola) with the same value in all six conditions, since} \new{$\beta$}\new{,} \new{$\delta$} \new{and} \new{${\sigma }_{H}$} \new{are properties of the participant and not of the condition. A cell with a higher rate has a smaller standardized distance and so a shallower predicted log-rate slope.}
\end{corollary}
}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{corollary}[Model 1 cannot ramp and activate at the same rate]\label{cor:tradeoff}
\new{Under Model 1 any delay-period ramp exists only by virtue of the non-stationarity of $H$, and it is traded off against the rate itself, the trade-off being carried by the standardized distance. Proof. Maintenance begins with the filtered signal at its target and the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck input at its own mean, so $H\left(\tau \right)=0$ with zero variance and the moments \eqref{eq:varH} and \eqref{eq:vardotH} relax to their stationary limits from below. Setting $\beta =0$ in \eqref{eq:nu2} and dropping the covariance term of \eqref{eq:MS} (which is non-negative, so it raises the rate at every distance without changing which distances give a rising one, and is retained in the numerical evaluation) gives $\ln \nu =\ln \left[\omega \left(\rho \right)/2\pi \right]-a{\left(\rho \right)}^{2}/2$ with $\omega \left(\rho \right)$ the characteristic frequency at elapsed time $\rho$ and $a\left(\rho \right)=d/{\sigma }_{H}\left(\rho \right)$ at constant $d$, and so}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd \left(\ln \nu \right)}{\dd \rho }=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{{\left({\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}\left(\rho \right)\right)}^{'}}{{\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}\left(\rho \right)}-\frac{{\left({\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(\rho \right)\right)}^{'}}{{\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(\rho \right)}\right]+\frac{{a}^{2}}{2}\frac{{\left({\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(\rho \right)\right)}^{'}}{{\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(\rho \right)}
\label{eq:slope2}
\end{equation}
}

\new{The second term is positive (the variance is still filling in) and is what produces the ramp; the first is negative throughout the analyzed window. Two consequences follow. A positive slope requires $a>1$ to good approximation, while by \eqref{eq:nu1} every further unit of standardized distance costs a factor ${e}^{-{a}^{2}/2}$ in the rate. Ramp and rate are therefore governed by the same quantity in opposite directions, and the largest ramp Model 1 can produce at a given rate is what the frontier of Results (Delay-locked) computes.}
\end{corollary}
}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{corollary}[Model 1 cannot hold a ramp to the response signal]\label{cor:decay}
\new{Under Model 1 the log-rate slope must decay across the analysis window. Proof. By \eqref{eq:slope2} the ramp is carried by the relative rate of variance growth, and since} \new{${\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(\rho \right)=\int_{0}^{\rho } {K}^{2}$} \new{that quantity has the exact form}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{equation*}
\frac{{\left({\sigma }_{H}^{2}\right)}^{'}\left(\rho \right)}{{\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(\rho \right)}=\frac{K{\left(\rho \right)}^{2}}{\int_{0}^{\rho } K{\left(v\right)}^{2}\, \dd v}
\end{equation*}
}

\new{It is decreasing in} \new{$\kappa$}\new{, and its supremum over} \new{$\kappa$} \new{is the limit} \new{$\kappa \to 0$}\new{, in which} \new{$K\left(v\right)\to {\sigma }_{\varepsilon }\left(1-{e}^{-\lambda v}\right)$} \new{and the equation above becomes} \new{$1/\left(\rho -3/\left(2\lambda \right)\right)$} \new{up to terms of order} \new{${e}^{-\lambda \rho }$}\new{: the filter's lag displaces the clock and nothing else survives. Hence} \new{${\left({\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(\rho \right)\right)}^{'}/{\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(\rho \right)\le 1/\left(\rho -3/\left(2\lambda \right)\right)$} \new{throughout, so no choice of} \new{$\kappa$} \new{makes the log-rate slope decay more slowly than that. The realized decay is steeper for two further reasons: the first bracket of \eqref{eq:slope2} is negative and grows in relative importance as the transient dies, and the standardized distance itself falls as the variance fills, shrinking the factor} \new{${a}^{2}/2$} \new{that multiplies the second. Model 2 is under no such constraint: by \eqref{eq:slope} its slope is proportional to a standardized distance falling linearly. The decay factors this imposes, and the observed one, are in Results.}
\end{corollary}
}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{proposition}[duration and the stimulus-period rate]\label{prop:duration}
\new{Under Model 1 the stimulus-period crossing rate is higher after the long stimulus, and at zero coherence this is carried entirely by the growth of the fluctuation. Fix every parameter, let the analysed window be $[0,T]$, and let $D_{s}<D_{l}$ satisfy $L+D_{s}<T\le L+D_{l}$, where $L:=L_{\mathrm{motion}}$. Write $\tau :=L+D_{s}$, $\rho :=t-\tau$, and let $\tilde{y}_{D}$ be the gate-facing signal under duration $D$ in the absence of the internal threshold. Then:}

\new{(i) $\tilde{y}_{s}$ and $\tilde{y}_{l}$ have the same law on $[0,\tau ]$;}

\new{(ii) for $t\in (\tau ,T]$, $\Var \tilde{y}_{l}(t)-\Var \tilde{y}_{s}(t)=\int_{0}^{\rho }\left[{\sigma }_{x}^{2}\left(1-e^{-\lambda (D_{s}+w)}\right)^{2}-K(w)^{2}\right]\, \dd w$;}

\new{(iii) if ${\sigma }_{x}\left(1-e^{-\lambda D_{s}}\right)\ge {\sigma }_{\varepsilon }\left(\kappa /\lambda \right)^{\kappa /(\lambda -\kappa )}$ then that difference is non-negative for every $\rho \ge 0$;}

\new{(iv) $\E \tilde{y}_{l}(t)-\E \tilde{y}_{s}(t)=skc\left[\rho -{\lambda }^{-1}\left(e^{-\lambda D_{s}}-e^{-\lambda (D_{s}+\rho )}\right)\right]\ge 0$, with equality if and only if $c=0$.}

\new{Under (iii) the standardized distance to the gate satisfies $a_{l}(t)\le a_{s}(t)$ for every $t$ in the window, at every coherence, including $c=0$, where (iv) contributes nothing.}

\new{\emph{Proof.} (i) On $[0,\tau ]$ encoded evidence is available in both conditions and the dynamics are identical, so driving both by the same $W_{x}$ makes the paths equal.}

\new{(ii) During accumulation, integrating $\dd y=\lambda (x-y)\, \dd t$ against $\dd x=skc\, \dd t+{\sigma }_{x}\, \dd W_{x}$ from $L$ and integrating by parts gives $\tilde{y}(L+w)=skc\left[w-{\lambda }^{-1}(1-e^{-\lambda w})\right]+{\sigma }_{x}\int_{0}^{w}\left(1-e^{-\lambda (w-u)}\right)\, \dd W_{x}(u)$, so}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{equation*}
\Var \tilde{y}(L+w)=V(w):={\sigma }_{x}^{2}\int_{0}^{w}{\left(1-e^{-\lambda v}\right)}^{2}\, \dd v={\sigma }_{x}^{2}\left[w-\frac{3}{2\lambda }+\frac{2}{\lambda }e^{-\lambda w}-\frac{1}{2\lambda }e^{-2\lambda w}\right]
\end{equation*}
}

\new{For $t>\tau $ the short condition maintains at $m=\tilde{y}_{s}\left(\tau \right)$ and $\tilde{y}_{s}(t)=m+H\left(\rho \right)$ with $H$ as in \eqref{eq:H}; $m$ is measurable with respect to $W_{x}$ and $H$ is driven by ${W}_{\varepsilon }\indep {W}_{x}$, so $\Var \tilde{y}_{s}(t)=V\left(D_{s}\right)+{V}_{H}\left(\rho \right)$ with ${V}_{H}\left(\rho \right)=\int_{0}^{\rho } K{\left(v\right)}^{2}\, \dd v$. The long condition is still accumulating, so $\Var \tilde{y}_{l}(t)=V\left(D_{s}+\rho \right)$. Subtracting and differentiating $V$ under the integral gives (ii).}

\new{(iii) The integrand in (ii) is non-negative for every $w\ge 0$ as soon as ${\sigma }_{x}\left(1-e^{-\lambda \left(D_{s}+w\right)}\right)\ge K\left(w\right)$, and $1-e^{-\lambda \left(D_{s}+w\right)}\ge 1-e^{-\lambda D_{s}}$, so it suffices that ${\sigma }_{x}\left(1-e^{-\lambda D_{s}}\right)\ge {\max }_{w\ge 0}K\left(w\right)$. Differentiating $K$ gives the maximizer ${w}^{*}=\ln \left(\lambda /\kappa \right)/\left(\lambda -\kappa \right)$ and $K\left({w}^{*}\right)={\sigma }_{\varepsilon }{\left(\kappa /\lambda \right)}^{\kappa /\left(\lambda -\kappa \right)}$.}

\new{(iv) From the mean in (ii), $\E \tilde{y}_{l}(t)-\E \tilde{y}_{s}(t)=skc\left[\rho -{\lambda }^{-1}e^{-\lambda D_{s}}\left(1-e^{-\lambda \rho }\right)\right]$, and ${\lambda }^{-1}\left(1-e^{-\lambda \rho }\right)\le \rho $ with $e^{-\lambda D_{s}}\le 1$. $\square $}
\end{proposition}
}

{\color{addcol}
\begin{remark}[the scope of the approximations]\label{rem:scope}
\new{The quantity computed above is the expected upcrossing rate of an unabsorbed Gaussian process, and the quantity measured is neither of those things exactly. Two idealizations separate them, and each makes the computed value an upper bound on the measured one.}

\new{The observable is a merged supra-threshold interval rather than an upcrossing, so crossings closer together than the merge interval are counted once. How often that happens is set by the characteristic frequency $\sqrt{{\sigma }_{\dot{H}}^{2}\left(t\right)/{\sigma }_{H}^{2}\left(t\right)}$. Wherever Model 1 has a ramp at all the characteristic period is far longer than the merge interval and successive crossings are essentially never merged; merging bites only at large $\kappa$, where the transient is over before the window opens and there is no ramp to bound.}

\new{The response criterion is an absorbing boundary: a delay-period crossing that reaches it ends the trial as a premature response, and conditioning on survival destroys the Gaussianity \eqref{eq:nu} rests on. Absorption removes preferentially the trials closest to the gate, and preferentially late in the window, so it depresses the late rate and lowers the measured slope below the computed one.}

\new{What is derived above therefore bounds what Model 1 can be measured to do; it does not predict the measurement exactly.}
\end{remark}
}

Writing the log-intensity as a linear function of time,} \new{$\ln \nu \left(t\right)={c}_{0}+{c}_{1}t$}\new{, the log-rate slope} \new{${c}_{1}$} \new{was estimated per participant and per coherence $\times$ duration cell from an inhomogeneous-Poisson point-process likelihood and entered into the same repeated-measures ANOVA as any other score (two-stage estimator); a one-stage log-linear model fitted across all participants with cluster-robust standard errors gave the same pattern of results (below). Both estimators treat the Poisson likelihood as a quasi-likelihood rather than a literal one, since upcrossing events cluster in time; standard errors accordingly come from between-participant variation (two-stage) or a cluster-robust correction (one-stage). This is a different estimator from the binned one the bound of the Appendix is compared against (Materials and Methods), which must match the binning of the published analysis; the two agree on the sign and the approximate size of the slope.}
