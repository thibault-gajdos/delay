

Recent theoretical and experimental works demonstrated that decision and actions are intimely intricated. Specifically, in situations where decisions prompt immediate actions (such as when driving and spotting a pedestrian about to cross the road), electrophysiological investigations of the motor system suggest that decision processes continuously flow to the motor structures that prepare and execute the response (O'Connell et al., 2018; O'Connell and Kelly, 2021; Servant et al., 2021; Weindel et al., 2021). These findings have laid the groundwork for integrated theoretical models of decisions and actions proposing that decision and motor execution are driven by common mechanisms (Verdonck et al. (2021), Dendaw et al. (2023)). 

Importantly, all continuous flow models we are aware of were elaborated and tested  within the context of decisions promptly followed by action. Whether the robust correlation between decision and action observed in these instances persists when actions are delayed is currently unknown. Exploring this issue holds dual significance. Firstly, despite regularly facing such scenarios (such as deciding on a presidential candidate), little is understood about their parallels and distinctions compared to immediate decisions. Secondly, examining these delayed decisions offers a natural avenue for scrutinizing continuous flow models and gaining deeper insights into their mechanisms.

The aim of the present study is precisely to fill that gap. The remaining of this introduction is structured as follows. First, we will briefly present a recent integrated  theoretical model of decision and action (GCD, Dendaw et al. (2023)), and propose a simple extension for postponed decisions. We will then present three experimental manipulations designed to explor potponned decisions in the light of the predictions of this model.
